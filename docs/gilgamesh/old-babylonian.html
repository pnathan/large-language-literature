Method

Five centuries before Sîn-lēqi-unninni’s twelve-tablet edition, the epic circulated in an Old Babylonian recension known today from fourteen tablets and fragments — two long tablets of a single old edition (Pennsylvania and Yale, whose series bore the incipit šūtur eli šarrī), and a scatter of school tablets and independent copies. All fourteen are printed here, in narrative order, from the electronic Babylonian Library’s critical editions (retrieved 26 August 2026 via the eBL API): 882 edited lines. The English is an original translation made against the Akkadian, sister to my rendering of the Standard edition and deliberately consistent with it, so that where the two texts share a verse, the translations differ only where the Akkadian does. The dialect shows its age: mimation intact, w- preserved, Huwawa for Humbaba, Sursunabu for Ur-šanabi. A Synopsis of three passages opens the file; each witness closes with notes reading it against the late edition.

Three passages, two recensions

1 · The ale-wife: sermon against sailing directions

The Old Babylonian Šiduri preaches the poem’s only carpe diem; the Standard edition hands the same woman a route-map and moves the theology downstream to Ūta-napišti. Set side by side, the deletion is visible as a decision.

2 · “Whatever he ever does is only wind”

The young king’s creed. The old version frames the couplet between heaven and the sun and ends with the wager — “if I fall, I will have planted my name.” The late version keeps the creed, cuts the frame, and lets the rest of the epic annul the wager.

3 · The dream of the meteor

Same dream, five centuries apart. The old telling is brisk and bodily (“it was too heavy for me”); the late telling is longer, stronger (“too strong for me”), and adds the crowd, the kissing of feet, and the mother’s full oracle of the friend.

Against the Standard version

OB III 140–143 vs SB II 232–235 — The core carpe-diem-of-glory speech. OB: 'Who ever went up to the sky? Only the gods dwell for ever with the sun; as for man — his days are numbered; whatever he ever does is only wind.' The SB keeps the couplet but strips the heaven-and-sun frame — the late poem hoards its sun-theology for Šamaš.

OB III 148 — šumma amtaqut šumī lū ušzīz — 'if I fall, I will have planted my name.' The OB hero buys fame with death cheerfully; the whole SB epic is the slow refutation of this line.

OB III 131–133 — Wēr — In the old version the forest's guardian-god is Wēr (a storm-god), with Huwawa as his appointee; the SB text deletes Wēr and makes Enlil the appointer (SB II 227–228).

OB III 165–171 — The forging: axes of three talents, blades of two, thirty minas of gold inlay, ten talents of kit per man — roughly 300 kg each. The SB parallel (II 247–253) keeps the scene but the numbers were already legend.

OB III 229–235 — Gilgameš weeps before his personal god and bargains like a client: 'I will build you a house of your delight.' The intimate contractual piety of the old period; the SB replaces it with Ninsun's grand liturgy to Šamaš (SB III).

VA+BM 1'–15' — Šamaš's rebuke — Unique to the old version: the sun-god himself tells the wanderer 'the life you keep hunting you will not find,' and Gilgameš answers with the poem's rawest lines — 'let my eyes look on the sun; when may a dead man ever see the sun's bright glare?' The SB deleted the exchange; its ghost is the interrogation formula of SB X.

VA+BM a+8 — ibrī-man itabbiam ana rigmīya — 'perhaps my friend will rise at my cry': the reason he kept the corpse seven days, spoken nowhere in the late text, where only the worm remains (SB X 59–61).

VA+BM a+18–a+30 — Šiduri's speech — The deleted sermon entire: death set aside for mankind, life kept in the gods' hands; the full belly, the daily feast, the clean clothes, the washed head, the child at the hand, the wife in the lap. The SB gave her sailing directions instead (SB X 79–91) and moved the theology to Ūta-napišti (SB X 297–322). See the Synopsis above.

VA+BM b+4 — Sursunabu — The boatman's older name (later Ur-šanabi); his master is Ūta-naʾištim, an older shape of Ūta-napišti. Even the proper names were still settling.

VA+BM b+22–b+27 — The Stone Ones as 'my ferrymen, so that I need not touch the Waters of Death' — their function stated plainly, where the SB (X 102–106) leaves it dark; and the punting-poles here number three hundred of willow(?), cut after the smashing, exactly as in SB X 160–166.
