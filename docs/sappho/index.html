Note on the text

These translations were made directly from Sappho’s ancient Greek witnesses — papyri and the ancient authors who quote her — fetched and cited from real primary sources, never from a model’s memory of the Greek and never by consulting an existing English translation. Every fragment below lists the witnesses that preserve it; where more than one survives, their variant readings are recorded rather than silently collapsed into one “best” text. Each translation was independently reviewed by a separate model for fidelity to the Greek and for any suspicious resemblance to a specific published translation.

Brackets and ellipses mark real gaps in the surviving text — nothing is invented to fill a lacuna. Translations aim to be faithful first, readable second: plain modern English carrying the sense and imagery, not a pastiche of any one translator’s style.

Fr. 1

Hymn/Ode to Aphrodite — the only poem of Sappho's transmitted essentially complete; preserved by quotation in Dionysius of Halicarnassus, On Literary Composition.

Ποικιλόθρον᾽ ἀθάνατ᾽ Ἀφροδίτα,Immortal Aphrodite of the shimmering throne,
παῖ Δίος δολόπλοκε, λίσσομαί σε,child of Zeus, weaver of wiles, I beg you,
μή μ᾽ ἄσαισι μηδ᾽ ὀνίαισι δάμνα,do not break my spirit, lady, with anguish
πότνια θῦμον:and pain,
ἀλλὰ τύδ᾽ ἔλθ᾽, αἴ ποκα κἀτέρωταbut come here, if ever before, at another time,
τᾶς ἔμας αὔδως ἀίοισα πήλυyou heard my voice from far off
ἔκλυες, πάτρος δὲ δόμον λίποισα,and listened, and leaving your father's house
χρύσιον ἦλθεςyou came, golden,
ἄρμ᾽ ὐπασδεύξαισα. κάλοι δέ σ᾽ ἆγονhaving yoked your chariot. Beautiful swift sparrows
ὠκέες στροῦθοι περὶ γᾶς μελαίναςbrought you above the black earth,
πύκνα διννῆντες πτέρ᾽ ἀπ᾽ ὠράνᾤθερος διὰ μέσσω.whirring their wings fast and thick, down from heaven through the middle air.
αἶψα δ᾽ ἐξίκοντο: τὺ δ᾽, ὦ μάκαιρα,Swiftly they arrived; and you, blessed one,
μειδιάσαισ᾽ ἀθανάτω προσώπωsmiling with your immortal face,
ἤρε᾽, ὄττι δἦν τὸ πέπονθα κὤττιasked what I had suffered this time, and why
δηὖτε κάλημι,I was calling you again,
κὤττ᾽ ἔμω μάλιστα θέλω γενέσθαιand what, in my maddened heart,
μαινόλᾳ θύμω: τίνα δηὖτε πείθωI most wanted to happen. "Whom shall I persuade
μαῖσ᾽ ἄγην ἐς σὰν φιλότατα; τίς σ᾽, ὦthis time to lead back into your love? Who, Sappho,
Ψάπφ᾽, ἀδικήει;wrongs you?
καὶ γὰρ αἰ φεύγει, ταχέως διώξει:For if she runs now, soon she will chase;
αἰ δὲ δῶρα μὴ δέκετ᾽, ἀλλὰ δώσει:if she refuses gifts, she will give them instead;
αἰ δὲ μὴ φίλει, ταχέως φιλήσειif she does not love, soon she will love,
κωὐκ ἐθέλοισα.even against her will."
ἔλθέ μοι καὶ νῦν, χαλεπᾶν δὲ λῦσονCome to me now too, and free me from
ἐκ μεριμνᾶν, ὄσσα δέ μοι τελέσσαιharsh cares, and all that my heart
θῦμος ἰμάρρει, τέλεσον, σὺ δ᾽ αὔταlongs to have done, accomplish it; and you yourself
σύμμαχος ἔσσο.be my ally in the fight.

Translator’s note. The manuscript tradition of Dionysius is genuinely uncertain at several points, but none change the overall sense much. I follow the Perseus/Radermacher text used here: "ὄττι δἦν τὸ πέπονθα" (line 14, "what I had suffered [this] time") rather than the variant "δηὖτε πέπονθα" found in Wharton/Cox (which would mean "suffered again" instead, echoing the "again" of the next line) — the difference is minor. I also render "πείθω" (line 17) as the verb "I persuade" (the goddess/Sappho asking whom she must win over) rather than treating it as the proper name of the goddess Peitho ("Persuasion"), following the majority/Perseus reading rather than Wharton's older capitalized "Πείθω." The whole final speech of Aphrodite (lines 17-23) is rendered in quotation marks for clarity, since Greek marks it only by sense, not punctuation.

Textual variants

All three fetched print recensions (Perseus/Radermacher; Wharton 1885; Cox 1924) transmit the SAME ancient witness — the quotation embedded in Dionysius of Halicarnassus, De Compositione Verborum 23 — so differences between them reflect (a) the real instability of Dionysius's own medieval manuscript tradition (sigla P, F, M, V etc., which disagree at several points) as resolved differently by each editor, and, in the two archive.org OCR scans, (b) some probable scanning noise. Line-by-line comparison against the Perseus/Radermacher text given above:

Line 1: Wharton and Cox both print "ἀθάνατ᾽" (agreeing with Perseus); a separate manuscript variant reported for this line in Dionysius's tradition (per the critical apparatus in J. M. Edmonds's 1922 Loeb, consulted but too OCR-corrupted to quote verbatim) is the poem's very first word: "ποικιλόθρον᾽" (richly-/rainbow-throned) is the majority reading, but some manuscripts of Dionysius read "ποικιλόφρον᾽" (many-wiled/subtle-minded) instead — a variant explicitly noted by Wharton too ("ποικιλόθρον᾽... is by some read ποικιλόφρον᾽").

Line 3: Perseus/Cox read "μηδ᾽ ὀνίαισι"; Wharton reads "μήτ᾽ ὀνίαισι" (μηδ᾽/μήτ᾽ — a genuine correlative-particle variant, not OCR noise, since both letter-sequences are equally legible).

Line 5: Perseus "τύδ᾽... αἴ ποκα"; Wharton/Cox "τυίδ᾽... αἴποτα/αἴποτὰ" — spelling/word-division variants for the same adverbs ("hither"; "if ever").

Line 9: Perseus reads the Aeolic-dialect form "ὐπασδεύξαισα" (from *ζεύγνυμι with the Aeolic -σδ- reflex); both Wharton and Cox instead print the Atticized "ὑποζεύξαισα" — a well-known crux in Sappho scholarship, since ὐπασδεύξαισα is the harder, more distinctively Aeolic reading.

Line 11: Perseus prints the crasis form "ὠράνᾤθερος" (ὠράνω + αἴθερος run together with a coronis); Wharton and Cox instead print the two words uncrased, "ὠράνω αἴθε-ρος/-ρας" (Wharton has "-ρας", Cox has "-ρος"). Perseus/Cox read "διὰ μέσσω"; Wharton reads "διὰ μίσσω" (μέσσω is the standard Doric/Aeolic form for "middle"; μίσσω is almost certainly a Wharton-scan OCR misreading of epsilon as iota rather than a real variant). Perseus reads "διννῆντες"; Wharton/Cox read "δινεῦντες" (a real orthographic variant of the participle "whirring/whirling").

Line 13: Cox reads "τὺ δ᾽" as "σὺ δ᾽" — a real pronoun variant (Aeolic τύ vs. the more familiar σύ) also visible against Perseus's "τὺ δ᾽".

Line 14: Perseus reads "ὄττι δἦν τὸ πέπονθα"; Wharton/Cox instead read "ὅττι/ὄττι δηὖτε πέπονθα" — a substantive variant (δἦν τὸ vs. δηὖτε) affecting the sense of "what I have suffered [again]".

Line 16: Perseus reads "κὤττ᾽ ἔμω μάλιστα"; Wharton/Cox read "κὤττι μοι μάλιστα" — pronoun-form variant (ἔμω vs. μοι).

Line 17: Perseus/Cox read "τίνα δηὖτε πείθω" (πείθω as the finite verb "I persuade"); Wharton instead capitalizes and prints "τίνα δεῦτε Πείθω" (treating Peitho, "Persuasion", as a personified goddess-figure) — an interpretive/textual variant with real consequences for how the line is construed, reflecting older (Bergk-era) editorial practice.

Line 26: Perseus reads "ἰμάρρει"; Wharton reads "ἱμέρρει"; Cox reads "ἰμμέρρει" — three different spellings of the same Aeolic verb-form ("[my heart] yearns/desires"), illustrating ongoing uncertainty in the manuscript/editorial tradition about this word's exact orthography.

A further crux reported in the Edmonds 1922 Loeb apparatus (not independently re-verified against a clean digital source because that scan's Greek OCR was too corrupted to quote reliably) concerns line 10: the transmitted manuscript reading "περὶ γᾶς μελαίνας" (which both Perseus and Wharton/Cox print, as above) was emended by some 20th-century editors to "πότι/προτὶ γᾶν μέλαιναν" on grounds of Homeric idiom; this is a conjectural EMENDATION rather than an attested manuscript variant, and is not reflected in any of the three texts actually quoted above.

No comparison against the papyrus (P.Oxy. 2288) or against Hephaestion's quotation could be made, since no verbatim text for either was recoverable from a fetched source in this session (see the caveats and the corresponding witness notes above).

Invocation of Aphrodite to a garden sanctuary; uniquely preserved on a potsherd (ostracon) rather than papyrus.

δεῦρύ μ' ἐκ Κρήτας ἐπ[ὶ τόνδ]ε ναῦονCome to me here from Crete, to this holy temple,

Translator’s note. Lines 11-12 are almost entirely lost on the ostracon (shown only as an open bracket in the source), so no words are supplied there. The final stanza's syntax is unusual: the Greek runs "there then you ... taking, Cypris ... pour" as one continuous imperative sentence (with a gap after "you"), rather than two separate clauses; I've rendered it as a single flowing command to match the ostracon's grammar, moving "into golden cups" to the end of the English sentence for readability while keeping all the same content. I followed the ostracon's own readings throughout rather than the shorter/variant wording quoted later by Hermogenes (who adds an explicit "wind" as subject and omits the rose-shade clause) or Athenaeus (who quotes the last stanza recast into a different grammatical frame, "Come, Cypris... pouring").

Textual variants

Stanza 2 (ostracon lines 5–8) vs. Hermogenes' quotation: - Ostracon: ἐν δ' ὔδωρ ψῦχρον κελάδει δι' ὔσδων μαλίνων, βρόδοισι δὲ παῖς ὀ χῶρος ἐσκίαστ', αἰθυσσομένων δὲ φύλλων κῶμα κατέρρει. - Hermogenes (per Cox 1924 transcription): ἀμφὶ δ᾽ ὔδωρ ψῦχρον ὤνεμος κελάδει δἰ ὔσδων μαλίνων, αἰθυσσομέμων δὲ φύλλων κῶμα κατάρρει. - Differences: (1) ἐν δ' (ostracon, "and in it...") vs ἀμφὶ δ' (Hermogenes, "and round about..."); (2) Hermogenes' text supplies an explicit subject ὤνεμος ("the wind") for κελάδει, absent from the ostracon, where the water itself is the subject; (3) Hermogenes' quotation entirely omits the ostracon's clause "βρόδοισι δὲ παῖς ὀ χῶρος ἐσκίαστ'" (roses shading the ground), passing directly from μαλίνων to αἰθυσσομέμων — i.e. Hermogenes quotes a shorter, non-continuous excerpt, not a variant reading of the full stanza; (4) κατέρρει (ostracon) vs κατάρρει (Hermogenes as printed by Cox) — likely an orthographic/accentual difference rather than a substantive one.

Stanza 4 (ostracon lines 13–16) vs. Athenaeus's quotation: - Ostracon: ἔνθα δὴ σύ . . . . ἔλοισα Κύπρι χρυσίαισιν ἐν κυλίκεσσιν ἄβρως ὀμμεμείχμενον θαλίαισι νέκταρ οἰνοχόαισον. - Athenaeus, Kaibel ms. text: ἐλθέ, Κύπρι, χρυσίαισιν ἐν κυλίκεσσιν ἁβρῶς συμμεμιγμένον θαλίαισι νέκταρ οἰνοχοοῦσα. - Differences: (1) Athenaeus's quotation opens with the plain imperative ἐλθέ, Κύπρι ("[Come], Cypris"), replacing the ostracon's ἔνθα δὴ σύ . . . . ἔλοισα Κύπρι construction (a participial clause built around "you, having taken..."); (2) ὀμμεμείχμενον (ostracon, Aeolic-dialect perfect passive participle) vs συμμεμιγμένον (Athenaeus, Attic/koine-dialect form of the same participle) — a genuine dialectal substitution; (3) οἰνοχόαισον (ostracon, 2nd-person imperative "pour!") vs οἰνοχοοῦσα (Athenaeus, feminine nominative present participle "pouring"), each grammatically fitted to its own sentence frame; (4) ἄβρως/ἁβρῶς is the same adverb differing only in breathing/accent as printed in the two sources consulted. - Additionally, the two modern print sources consulted for the Athenaeus quotation itself disagree editorially: Kaibel's manuscript-based text (ἁβρῶς, οἰνοχοοῦσα) vs Cox's 1924 Aeolic-restored text (ἄβραις, οἰνοχόεισα) — see witnesses 3–4.

Translator’s note. Source verification passed: id and note matched the expected values. The papyrus (P.Oxy. 7) is heavily lacunose — roughly 80% of the running text is modern editorial restoration in brackets, and three editors (Blass 1898, Edmonds 1922, Campbell/Lobel-Page-Voigt) diverge on several key readings. Textual variants

Only one ancient source survives for this fragment (the single papyrus P.Oxy. 1231, fr. 1 col. I + fr. 3), so there is no independent second ancient copy — but the papyrus is so damaged that successive modern editors have restored/read the gaps differently, producing real, substantive variant texts of the same lacunae. Key crux, ll.9-10 (numbering as in the papyrus column):

(1) Grenfell & Hunt, editio princeps (P.Oxy. X, 1914): κα[ί σ]ε πι[κροτέρ]αν ἔπευρ[εν ('...found you more bitter' — comparative adjective, 3rd sg. AORIST INDICATIVE ἔπευρεν) ... [οἱ] δὲ καυχάσαντο τόδ᾽ ἐννέ[ποντες ('and THEY boasted, saying this' — 3rd pl. indicative, plural subject).

(2) Edmonds, Lyra Graeca (1922), fr.37: κα[ί σ]ε πι[κροτέρ]αν ἐπεύρ[οι (same comparative πικροτέραν as Grenfell & Hunt, but AORIST OPTATIVE ἐπεύροι, 'may she find you more bitter') ... [μη]δὲ καυχάσαιτο τόδ᾽ ἐννέ[ποισα ('nor may SHE boast, saying' — 3rd sg. optative + fem. sg. participle, singular subject) — a hybrid between the other two readings.

(3) Modern standard text (Campbell 1982 / Voigt-type, per Digital Sappho / Haverford): κα[ί σ]ε πι[κροτάτ]α̣ν ἐπεύρ[οι ('may she find you most bitter' — SUPERLATIVE πικροτάταν, not comparative, aorist optative ἐπεύροι) ... μη]δὲ καυχάσ[α]ιτο τόδ᾽ ἐννέ[ποισα (same as Edmonds' reading here).

So across the tradition there are three distinct restorations of the adjective (πικροτέραν 'more bitter' in 1914 and Edmonds 1922, vs. πικροτάταν 'most bitter' in the later standard text), two distinct restorations of the verb (indicative ἔπευρεν in 1914 vs. optative ἐπεύροι in Edmonds and the standard text), and two distinct restorations of the following clause's grammatical subject/number (plural indicative οἱ δὲ καυχάσαντο ... ἐννέποντες in 1914, i.e. unnamed people boasting, vs. singular optative μηδὲ καυχάσαιτο ... ἐννέποισα in Edmonds and the standard text, i.e. a prayer that Doricha herself NOT be able to boast). The 1914 princeps' ll.9-10 restorations are explicitly credited there to Wilamowitz-Moellendorff ('Restored by W-M'), and Doricha's name in l.11 is also credited to Wilamowitz-Moellendorff's recognition, cross-checked there against Posidippus' epigram on Doricha quoted from Athenaeus.

A further, smaller variant at l.5: Campbell/Digital-Sappho restores the line-end as ὄσσα δὲ πρ]όσθ᾽ [ἄμ]βροτε κῆ[να λῦσαι ('...to atone for/undo those things', infinitive), whereas the compilation site inamidst.com's presentation of the same lacuna gives [ὄσσα δὲ πρ]όσθ᾽[ ἄμ]βροτε κῆ[ν᾽ ἔλυσεν] ('...undid those things', 3rd sg. aorist indicative) — again a comparative/indicative-vs-infinitive style divergence in how editors have filled the same gap.

Orthographic/accentual micro-variants also appear between recensions of ll.11-12 (e.g. ὡς vs ὠς; [εἰς] bracketed identically in all).

Textual variants

Because fr. 17 survives only on papyrus (no ancient author is known to quote it as a literary testimonium), the "variants" here are of two kinds: (A) actual differences of letters/accentuation between the papyrus witnesses themselves, and (B) the very large difference between the pre-2014 conjectural reconstruction (Lobel–Page/Voigt, built almost entirely from meter and guesswork for the right-hand halves of lines) and the post-2014 reconstructions once P.GC. inv. 105 supplied real papyrus letters for those halves.

(A) Witness-level variants noted in Burris–Fish–Obbink, ZPE 189 (2014): - Line 3 (17.3): PSI 123 reads ερτον (ἐρᾰτον) where P.Oxy. 1231 and P.GC. inv. 105 agree on ἀράταν — flagged by the editors as a metrically incorrect alternative in the PSI 123 tradition. - Accentuation: of P.GC. inv. 105's seven clear accent marks, none coincides with an accent in the other witnesses at any point of overlap. P.GC. inv. 105 alone accents τόι at 17.4; P.Oxy. 1231 alone accents τύ at 17.7 and κάτ at 17.12 (both left unaccented, or the accent-bearing word differently divided, in P.GC. inv. 105). - Line 6 (17.6): the exact spelling is debated — Burris–Fish–Obbink print πὲρ Εἴ[λιον] (a spelling paralleled in P.Ryl.Gr. 22), on the basis of the P.Oxy. 2289 fr. 9 traces, against Page's earlier Ἴλιον; Ferrari's 2014 edition instead prints πὲρ Εἴ[λειον]. - Line 12 (17.12): Burris–Fish–Obbink print κὰτ τὸ π[άλ][αον] (πάλαον, comparing the spelling παλάων at Alcaeus fr. 169a.5), while Ferrari's edition prints the more familiar κὰτ τὸ πάλ[αιον]. - Line 16 (17.16): Burris–Fish–Obbink leave μέτρ’ ὀλ[ open, tentatively suggesting the verb ὀλολύσδην (comparing Alcaeus fr. 130a.18–20 on the ritual ὀλολυγή of women at the same shrine); Ferrari's edition instead commits to the noun μέτρ' ὀλ̣[ολύγαϲ. - Line 20 (17.20, final preserved word): Ferrari prints [Ἦ]ρ’ ἀπίκε[ϲθαι in full; Burris–Fish–Obbink leave the line-opening more open, ]ρ’ ἀπικε[. - Crux at Ἀτρ[εϊδα]ι (line 3): the papyrus letters are compatible with either nominative plural Ἀτρέϊδαι ("the sons of Atreus," i.e. both Agamemnon and Menelaus) or dative singular Ἀτρεΐδᾳ ("for/to the son of Atreus," i.e. Menelaus alone); Burris–Fish–Obbink's commentary sets out both possibilities at length without deciding definitively, noting it bears on whether Sappho's version of the myth put one or both Atreidae on Lesbos.

Two recensions of the same single papyrus (P.Oxy. 1231) were located; no independent quoting-author testimonium (Athenaeus, Hephaestion, a lexicographer, etc.) for this poem turned up in searching.

The 'he seems to me equal to the gods' poem — first-person catalog of physical symptoms on watching a beloved woman with a man; quoted whole by [Longinus], On the Sublime.

Textual variants

Because the poem survives through one quoted exemplar read differently by successive editors, the recensions above diverge at several well-known cruces. Line references are to the stanza layout above (stanza.line).

(1) NUMBERING: This fragment is Bergk's/Wharton's/Edmonds's "fr. 2" but the now-standard citation, used by Lobel-Page (1955), Voigt (1971), Campbell (Loeb 1982), aoidoi.org, Digital Sappho and Wikipedia, is "fr. 31" (Lobel-Page/Voigt numbering, which incorporates the papyrus finds and renumbered the whole corpus). Both numbers denote the same poem.

(2) 1.2 ὅστις/ὄστις/ὄττις: Rhys Roberts prints the Atticized ὅστις (rough breathing, single τ); Wharton/Bergk ὄστις (smooth breathing, single τ); Edmonds and the Lobel-Page-based text both give the fully Aeolic geminate ὄττις.

(3) 1.3 ἱζάνει/ἰζάνει/ἰσδάνει: Rhys Roberts and Wharton print ἰζάνει/ἱζάνει (ζ); Edmonds also ἰζάνει; the aoidoi (Moore/Campbell) and Digital Sappho (Lobel-Page) texts print the alternative Aeolic spelling ἰσδάνει (σδ for ζ) — an orthographic rather than substantive difference.

(4) 1.3 πλησίον/πλάσιον: Rhys Roberts alone prints the Atticized πλησίον (η); all four other recensions print the Aeolic πλάσιον (ᾱ).

(5) 2.1 τό μοι μὰν / τό μοι μάν / τὸ δὴ 'μαν / τό μ' ἦ μάν: this word-group is the first major crux. Rhys Roberts and Wharton/Bergk independently agree on τό μοι μάν(v); aoidoi (Moore/Campbell) and Digital Sappho (Lobel-Page) independently agree on τό μ' ἦ μάν; Edmonds prints τὸ δὴ 'μαν, explicitly following the 19th-century philologist Ahrens's conjecture, and states in his apparatus (n.1, p.186) that "the mss[read] μὴ μάν."

(6) 2.2 καρδίαν ... ἐπτόασεν / ἐπτόησεν / κάρζαν ... ἐπεπτόασεν: Rhys Roberts and Wharton/Bergk both read καρδίαν ... ἐπτόασεν; aoidoi (Moore/Campbell) and Digital Sappho (Lobel-Page) both read καρδίαν ... ἐπτόησεν; Edmonds substitutes the rare word κάρζαν for καρδίαν and prints ἐπεπτόασεν, crediting the conjecture to Robortelli/himself and stating in his apparatus (n.2) that "mss [read] καρδίαν ἐν στήθεσσιν (-εσιν) ἐπτόασεν (corr. in one [ms] to ἐποπτόασεν)" — i.e. even Edmonds's own apparatus confirms the plain manuscript reading is καρδίαν ... ἐπτόασεν, with one manuscript instead correcting to ἐποπτόασεν.

(7) 3.1–3.2 (the "briefly I see you" line) — the most contested crux in the poem: Rhys Roberts prints ὥς σε γὰρ ἴδω βροχέως, με φωνᾶς; Wharton/Bergk prints ὡς γὰρ εὔιδον βροχέως σε, φώνας; aoidoi (Moore/Campbell) and Digital Sappho (Lobel-Page) both print ὡς/ὠς γὰρ εἰσίδω βροχέως σε, φώνας. Edmonds diverges most sharply, splitting the adverb βροχέως into a vocative proper name addressed to the beloved, "Βρόχε(α)" (a supposed pet-name, from βραχύς), printing ὡς γὰρ ἔς τ' ἴδω, Βρόχε', ὤς με φώνας — his footnote (n.1, p.187) glosses this as "(or Brochea) dimin. of a compd. of βραχύς," and his footnote on the same line (n.3) states "mss [read] ὥς γ[ὰρ] σίδω βρόχεως (βροχέως) κ.τ.λ.," i.e. Edmonds's own apparatus shows the manuscript reading is the corrupt σίδω against a plain adverb βρόχεως, and that his "Βρόχε'"-as-name reading is his own idiosyncratic construal, not shared by any other edition consulted here.

(8) 4.2 ὑπαδεδρόμακεν / ὐπαδεδρόμηκεν: Rhys Roberts, Wharton/Bergk, and Edmonds all print the (unaugmented) perfect ὑπαδεδρόμακεν/ὐπαδεδρόμακεν (differing only in breathing); aoidoi (Moore/Campbell) and Digital Sappho (Lobel-Page) print the augmented ὑπαδεδρόμηκεν/ὐπαδεδρόμηκεν.

(9) 5.1 κὰδ δέ μ' ἴδρως κακχέεται / ἀ δέ μ' ἴδρως κακχέεται / ψῦχρος-variants: Rhys Roberts alone keeps κάδ (= κατά, "down"); Wharton/Bergk, Edmonds, aoidoi and Digital Sappho all print ἀ/καδ instead (a feminine article/demonstrative before ἴδρως). More importantly, aoidoi's apparatus (n.13, citing its two base editions by name) records: "κακχέεται Moore : ψῦχρος ἔχει Campbell" — i.e. Moore (1947) prints κακχέεται ("pours down") where Campbell (1967) prints the different phrase ψῦχρος ἔχει ("cold holds/seizes") at this same point. Independently, Edmonds's apparatus (n.6, p.187) reports that an editor he abbreviates "Long." "apparently read κὰδ δ' ἴδρως ψῦχρος χέεται," reporting that editor's manuscripts as giving "ἐκ δὲ (ἐκαδε) μ' ἰδ. ψ. κακχέεται," and Edmonds argues μοι is grammatically necessary. All three of these independent apparatus notes (aoidoi/Moore/Campbell; Edmonds/"Long.") converge on the same real crux: this line is corrupt in the manuscript(s), and editors differ over whether it read a form of κακχέεται alone or involved ψῦχρος ("cold").

(10) 5.2 ἀγρεῖ / ἄγρει / ἄγρη: minor verb-ending variation across the same word (Rhys Roberts ἀγρεῖ; Wharton, aoidoi, Digital Sappho ἄγρει; Edmonds ἄγρη).

(11) 5.3 ᾽πιδεύην / 'πιδεύϝην / 'πιδεύης: Rhys Roberts and Wharton both give (ὀλίγω) ᾽πιδεύην/'πιδεύης without a digamma; aoidoi and Digital Sappho give 'πιδεύης; Edmonds alone marks a digamma, 'πιδεύϝην, and his apparatus (n.7, p.187) reports "mss [give] πιδευσην, πιδευην, or πιδευκην" — i.e. he documents three different actual manuscript spellings/corruptions at this word.

(12) End of poem — the text becomes lacunose/uncertain in every recension, and this is where the divergence is greatest: Rhys Roberts prints continuous ἀλλὰ πᾶν τολματόν, ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα ... with no brackets; Wharton/Bergk prints ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον, [ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα]. with the last three words bracketed as a supplement; aoidoi gives only ἀλλὰ πᾶν τόλματον ... and stops, noting in prose (not apparatus number) that "the last part of the fourth strophe is completely muddled... I have omitted what is probably the final word of the strophe," and that a following three-word "fifth strophe" (not shown in the fetched excerpt) "may not belong in this poem at all, but [is] included in standard editions." Edmonds diverges completely, printing πάντ<α νῦν τ>ολμάτε', ἐπεὶ πένησα — an imperative "τολμάτε'" governing a new syntax — and his apparatus (n.8, p.187) states "mss [give] ἀ. παντόλματον ἐ. (ἐ. καί) πένητα," i.e. the actual manuscript reading (confirmed independently by Rhys Roberts's plain text ἀλλὰ πᾶν τολματόν/παντόλματον ἐπεὶ καὶ πένητα) is close to what Rhys Roberts and Wharton print, and Edmonds's rewriting to a 2nd-person imperative is his own emendation against that manuscript reading.

Textual variants

Two genuinely independent ancient witnesses exist, and they do not overlap in wording at all — Eustathius quotes verse-text, Julian only paraphrases a single epithet — so the 'variants' below are of two different kinds: (A) real transmissional variants within/around the Eustathius quotation, and (B) the editorial reconciliation modern scholars perform to combine the two witnesses into one composite fragment.

(A) Within the Eustathius quotation: 1. The adverb before the verb (meaning roughly 'straightway'/'back again') is transmitted unstably across manuscripts and printed editions of Eustathius' Commentary: forms reported in modern apparatus include ἂψ, αἶψ, and ἆιψ. My OCR of the Stallbaum-edition scan rendered this word illegibly/corruptly (as 'dy'), so I could not independently resolve which of these three the scan actually prints; this should be checked against a clean facsimile or a modern critical apparatus (e.g. Voigt's or Campbell's) rather than relied on from my OCR pass. 2. Eustathius' own quoted Greek is markedly less 'Aeolic' than the form usually printed as Sappho fr. 34 in modern editions (Bergk, Wharton, Lobel-Page, Voigt, Campbell): Eustathius' text gives ἀποκρύπτουσι, ὁπότ᾽ ἄν, καλάν σελάναν (single -ν-), φαεινὸν εἶδος; the standard modern critical text instead prints the Aeolicized forms ἀπυκρύπτοισι, ὄπποτα, κάλαν σελάνναν (geminate -νν-), φάεννον εἶδος. This is a systematic editorial normalization to Sappho's Aeolic dialect, not something attested word-for-word in Eustathius himself — i.e. the 'Sapphic-looking' Aeolic text everyone quotes is itself a modern scholarly reconstruction laid over Eustathius' more Attic/koine-flavored quotation.

(B) Reconciling Eustathius and Julian: 3. Eustathius' quotation ends cleanly after γᾶν ('earth'/'land'), with nothing following in his text. Julian never quotes any Sapphic verse-words at all — he only states, in his own prose and indirect discourse, that Sappho called the moon ἀργυρέαν ('silver'). Modern composite editions (represented here by Wharton 1885) splice the two together: they keep Eustathius' three lines verbatim (Aeolicized), then add the bracketed conjecture [ἐπὶ πᾶσαν] and insert the word ἀργυρία (the Aeolic equivalent of Julian's ἀργυρέαν) into an assumed metrical lacuna after γᾶν, marking the surrounding gap with meter-filler symbols. Neither addition is transmitted continuous text from any single ancient source; it is an editorial reconstruction (traced by Wharton to a suggestion of Bishop Blomfield) built by combining the two independent witnesses. 4. Fragment numbering differs by edition: Bergk (Poetae Lyrici Graeci) and Wharton (following Bergk) both number this fragment '3'; the Bidez–Cumont apparatus to Julian likewise cites it as 'Sapph. Fr. 3' (i.e. Bergk's numbering); modern standard numbering (Diehl, Lobel–Page, Voigt, Campbell/Loeb) instead calls it fragment 34 — the number given in the task prompt.

Textual variants

The only ancient witnesses to Sappho fr. 44 are two papyrus copies from Oxyrhynchus; no ancient literary quotation (testimonium in Athenaeus, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Pseudo-Longinus, Hephaestion, Strabo, etc.) of any part of this poem is known to survive — it is a purely papyrological fragment.

(1) P.Oxy. 1232 (ed. pr. Grenfell & Hunt, P.Oxy. One genuine crux is documented at the line corresponding to composite line 31 ('the older/elder women ...'): the standard modern critical vulgate (Lobel-Page 1955, Voigt 1971, followed by Campbell 1982/digitalsappho.org and by inamidst.com's presentation of the Lobel-Page/Voigt text) prints γύναικες δ' ἐλέλυσδον ὄσαι προγενέστερα[ι — a verb built on the root ἐλελ- (cf. the cry ἐλελεῦ). Textual variants

There is only ONE ancient witness to this fragment: Maximus of Tyre (2nd c. CE), Or. 18.9 Hobein (= Or. XXIV.9 in the older Reiske/Dübner numbering Bergk used). No papyrus preserves this fragment, and no second ancient quoting author is known to cite it. Maximus embeds the Sappho reminiscence inside his own prose sentence, in indirect/adapted form (3rd person, with the definite article τὰς φρένας, and the ordinary aorist participle ἐμπεσών), rather than quoting Sappho's original Aeolic lyric verse verbatim. All differences below are therefore differences of EDITORIAL RECENSION/RESTORATION between modern scholars trying to recover Sappho's presumed original wording from Maximus's paraphrase, not differences between independent ancient sources:

1. Primary/manuscript-based text of Maximus himself (Hobein-tradition critical edition, via Perseids CTS): "ἐτίναξεν τὰς φρένας, ὡς ἄνεμος κατ᾿ ὄρος δρυσὶν ἐμπεσών" — left essentially as Maximus wrote it (3rd person, prose participle ἐμπεσών, manuscript reading κατ᾿ ὄρος retained against Nauck's rejected conjecture καταρρής).

2. Bergk (Poetae Lyrici Graeci), fr. 42, printed text: "(Ἔρος δαύτ᾽ ἐτίναξεν ἔμοι φρένας). / ἄνεμος κατ᾽ ὄρος δρύσιν ἐμπέσων." — restores 1st person ("ἔμοι" = "my", supplies "Ἔρος δαῦτ(ε)" = "Eros again"), drops the connective ὡς (which Bergk judged an addition by Maximus himself, "a sophista additum"), keeps the ordinary participle ἐμπέσων. - Bergk ALSO offers, in his apparatus only (not printed as main text), a rival reconstruction in pentameter word order: "Ἔρος δ᾽ ὡς ἄνεμος κατ᾽ ὄρος δρύσιν ἐμπέσων φρένας ἀμμετέρας ἐτίναξεν" (using Aeolic ἀμμετέρας, "our", instead of ἔμοι/μοι). - He also records J. 3. Wharton (1885/1895) and Cox (1924), following Bergk's restoration and numbering (fr. 42 / fr. 40 respectively): "Ἔρος δαῦτ᾽/δαὖτ᾽ ἐτίναξεν ἔμοι φρένας, / ἄνεμος κατ᾽ ὄρος δρύσιν ἐμπέσων." Order/enjambment also differs slightly (δ᾿ ἐτίναξέ μοι φρένας, run across the line-break, vs. Bergk's ἐτίναξεν ἔμοι φρένας on a single line).

ἠράμαν μὲν ἔγω σέθεν Ἂτθι πάλαι πότα·Atthis, I was in love with you once, long ago —
σμίκρα μοι παίς ἔμμεν᾿ ἐφαίνεο κἄχαρις.to me you seemed a tiny child, with no charm in you at all.

Translator’s note. The two lines are a modern editorial join (Lobel): line 1 is transmitted only by Hephaestion (as a bare metrical example), line 2 only by Plutarch (in a discussion of erotic charis between women). No ancient source attests them as a single couplet, though the pairing is standard in modern editions (Lobel-Page, Voigt, Campbell). Revised from the prior draft to move away from wording that tracked Campbell's Loeb too closely: μέν is left untranslated (its answering δέ clause is lost), πάλαι πότα is rendered with the doubled temporal sense "once, long ago," σμίκρα is now "tiny" rather than "small," and κἄχαρις (= καὶ ἄχαρις, "and without charm/grace") is unpacked as a separate clause, "with no charm in you at all," rather than a compound adjective, to vary both diction and syntax from the earlier version.

Textual variants

The single most important 'variant' here is structural, not orthographic: Sappho fr. 49 as printed in modern editions (Lobel-Page, Voigt, Campbell, and the digitalsappho.org text quoted above) is a MODERN EDITORIAL COMPOSITE. Line 1 (ἠράμαν μὲν ἔγω σέθεν, Ἄτθι, πάλαι πότα) and line 2 (σμίκρα μοι παῖς ἔμμεν ἐφαίνεο κἄχαρις) are each independently transmitted by a DIFFERENT single ancient witness -- Hephaestion quotes only line 1 (as a bare metrical specimen, with no surrounding context), and Plutarch quotes only line 2 (in the context of a philosophical dialogue on love). No ancient source quotes both lines together or attests that they form a single couplet from the same poem; the two-line unit is Edgar Lobel's editorial join. A search result for an academia.edu article titled 'What Lobel Hath Joined Together: Sappho 49 LP' (not directly fetched/verified by me) appears to argue precisely this point -- that the two half-lines may not even belong to the same book of Sappho's poetry (one search snippet suggested 49b might belong to Book 5 rather than Book 2) -- so this should be treated as a flagged secondary-literature claim, not independently confirmed by me.

Fr. 55

Polemic addressed to an uncultivated woman, warning of oblivion after death; transmitted by Stobaeus.

κατθάνοισα δὲ κείσῃ οὐδέ ποτα μναμοσύνα σέθενBut when you have died you will lie there, and no memory of you
ἔσσετ' οὐδὲ πόθα εἰς ὔστερον· οὐ γὰρ πεδέχῃς βρόδωνwill ever exist, not even afterward — for you have no share of the roses
τὼν ἐκ Πιερίας, ἀλλ' ἀφάνης κἀν Ἀίδα δόμῳthat come from Pieria; but unseen, even in the house of Hades,
φοιτάσῃς πεδ' ἀμαύρων νεκύων ἐκπεποταμένα.you will drift among the shadowy dead, flown out from among them.

Translator’s note. Followed the fullest witness (Stobaeus, as printed by Voigt) rather than the shorter Plutarch quotations or Cox's conjectural rewriting of the corrupt "ποκ'/πόθα... ὔστερον" clause; that word is textually uncertain (obelized by editors) but the general sense "not even afterward/ever again" is not in serious doubt. Translator’s note. Textual variants

Two independent ancient witnesses preserve non-overlapping portions of what modern editors print together as a single 2-line fragment (Sappho fr. 104a Voigt / Lobel-Page):

1. Extent of text quoted: Demetrius (On Style §141) quotes only the 'catalogue' half -- 'Ἕσπερε, πάντα φέρεις, φέρεις ὄϊν, φέρεις αἶγα, φέρεις ματέρι παῖδα' -- because his point is the rhetorical figure of anaphora (repeated φέρεις). The Etymologicum Magnum quotes only the opening half -- 'Ἔσπερε πάντα φέρων ὅσα φαίνολις ἐσκέδασ᾽ Αὔως' -- because its point is lexicographical (the meaning of the Aeolic word Αὔως, 'dawn'). Neither ancient witness by itself quotes the full two-line unit familiar from modern editions; that combined text is a modern editorial conflation of the two testimonia (attested already in Wharton 1887 and Edmonds 1922, both citing Bergk as the scholar who first joined them).

2. φέρεις vs. φέρων: Demetrius's manuscript tradition (per Roberts' 1902 Greek text) has the finite verb φέρεις ('you bring') opening the address, matching the anaphora of the following φέρεις...φέρεις...φέρεις. The Etymologicum Magnum instead has the participle φέρων ('bringing'), agreeing with the vocative Ἕσπερε/Ἔσπερε, which is metrically and grammatically necessary if the sentence continues (as EM's does) into a relative clause ὅσα φαίνολις ἐσκέδασ᾽ Αὔως ('as many things as shining Dawn scattered').

3. ματέρι vs. (ἄπυ) μάτερι: Demetrius's text as edited by Roberts reads plainly ματέρι παῖδα ('[you bring] the child to [its] mother') with no preposition. Modern composite editions (Wharton, Edmonds) print ἄπυ μάτερι παῖδα ('[you bring] the child home/away to its mother'), inserting the Aeolic preposition ἄπυ; on the evidence of Edmonds's own apparatus (as far as legible) this word's presence/form was itself disputed among the manuscripts/editors of Demetrius, with a conjecture attributed to Finckh -- i.e. it is not a straightforwardly attested reading in either of the two primary witnesses I was able to verify directly, but an editorially supplied or emended element of the conflated modern text.

4. Breathing on Ἕσπερε/Ἔσπερε: the clean Perseus/Roberts text of Demetrius shows the word with rough breathing (Ἕσπερε), the ordinary Attic/Koine spelling of 'evening/evening-star.' My reading of the Etymologicum Magnum scan appears to show smooth breathing (Ἔσπερε), which would be the expected Aeolic (psilotic) spelling Sappho herself would have used. I flag this only tentatively (see caveats) because breathing marks are the diacritic most easily lost or corrupted in this particular OCR scan, so I cannot certify this as a confirmed variant rather than a scanning artifact.

Together, (1)-(3) form a single coherent editorial fault-line: the psilotic/Aeolic-looking forms (σελάννα, ἔρχετ', κατεύδω) are Bergk's 19th-century emendations meant to make the fragment's dialect consistent with Sappho's Lesbian/Aeolic vernacular (psilosis = loss of aspiration), while the aspirated forms (σελάνα or σελάννα with single-ν, ἔρχεθ', καθεύδω) are closer to what Hephaestion's manuscripts actually transmit. Editors who accept Sapphic authorship (Voigt, Campbell, Edmonds, Rayor & Lardinois, Neri) print the emended/Aeolicized text; Page, who classifies the poem as anonymous (adespoton), keeps the manuscripts' non-Aeolic forms, which is itself part of his argument against Sapphic authorship.

Two independent fetched transcriptions of the P.Sapph.Obbink text were compared line-by-line (digitalsappho.org vs. CHS Classical Inquiries/Nagy) and are identical in wording except for:

Textual variants

The two ancient papyrus witnesses are not independent poems but overlapping copies/portions of the same text, and P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16 for the most part CONFIRMS rather than contradicts P. Sapph. Obbink; the "variants" are mainly (a) places where one papyrus preserves letters the other does not, and (b) a couple of genuine cruces of interpretation:

- Line 1 (Kypris-Poem numbering): P. Sapph. Obbink gives θαμε ̣ ̣ (articulated θαμέωϲ by Obbink); P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16.1 independently gives ]θαμε ̣[, which earlier editors (working from P. Oxy. 1231 alone) read as θαμέω[ν (Hunt) or θαμέω[ϲ (Diehl¹) — i.e. the new papyrus is used by Obbink to help decide between these earlier competing supplements to the *old* fragment. - Line 2: P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16.2 gives ]τ'τινα[, read by Hunt as ὄ]ττινα[ϲ γὰρ — a different case-ending/continuation than Obbink's ὄττινα δὴ φιλείη from the new papyrus; the new papyrus's ὄττινα (accusative singular, not plural ὄττινας) is preferred by Obbink, effectively overriding Hunt's old restoration. - Line 3: P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16.3 gives ]λιϲταπα[; Obbink notes this rules out an earlier proposed identification of these lines with a quotation in Etymologicum Magnum 449.34, because that quotation lacks the letters πα found here in both papyri. - Line 4: P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16.4 has NO letters at this point ("nothing here but blank papyrus"), because its column was laid out with a shorter adonean line at this position — i.e. an apparent structural/layout difference between the two copies at this line, not a wording variant. - Line 6: P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16.6 gives ]·γονωμ[ with a raised punctuation point that Obbink says may "counterindicate" reading the sequence as continuous ...αντιγονω..., i.e. the older papyrus's punctuation is used as evidence AGAINST one candidate reading of the newer papyrus. - Lines 7 and 9: P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16.7 (]αι μ'ουπρ[) and fr. 16.9 (]αι, blank; and separately ]ϲέ·θελω[) are used by Obbink to confirm and extend the new papyrus's traces (]μ' οὐ πρ- and ]ϲέ, θέλω[ respectively). - Lines 10–15 (old Voigt numbering)/30–33 (Obbink's continuous numbering): only P. Oxy. 1231 preserves letters at all here (the new papyrus is lost/illegible past line 29/9); within that stretch, lines 11–12 are not securely read from either papyrus but are supplied instead from the quotation in Apollonius Dyscolus, De Pronominibus — i.e. for those two lines the wording rests on a testimonium rather than on either papyrus.

Overall Obbink's stated conclusion (quoted verbatim from the article) is that the new papyrus "substantially augments Sappho fr. 26 Voigt (as previously known from P. Oxy. 1231 fr. 16), previous reconstructions of which it corrects as it invites new ones" — i.e. the "variant" at the level of the whole fragment is really a revision of prior scholarly reconstruction (Hunt's, Diehl's) rather than a disagreement between the two papyri themselves, which the editor treats as copies of the same underlying text.
