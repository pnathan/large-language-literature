# Expert Review: "The Guest House" Fresh Translation

**Reviewer**: Fable (per `FABLE-REVIEW-REQUEST.md`)
**Date**: 2026-08-26
**Verdict**: **Not ready for publication.** The core thesis is sound and worth pursuing, but the draft contains a category error about the source language, a misquoted opening line, an undisclosed splice of two non-contiguous passages, two substantive mistranslations, and several incorrect transliterations. All are fixable; none should survive into print.

---

## 0. One correction before anything else: this is not Middle Persian

All three documents describe the source as "Middle Persian." **Rumi did not write in Middle Persian.** Middle Persian (Pahlavi) is the language of Sasanian Iran, roughly 3rd–9th centuries CE, written in Pahlavi script — the language of Zoroastrian texts like the *Bundahišn*. Rumi (13th century) wrote **Classical New Persian** (also "Classical Persian" or "early New Persian"), the same language stage as Ferdowsi, Sa'di, and Hafez, written in Arabic script. The phrase "Middle Persian" appears in the research doc, the review request, and the translation's closing checklist; every instance must be changed to "Classical Persian." For a project whose stated purpose is philological rigor against popularizers, this single error would discredit the whole publication on sight.

Relatedly: the research doc's parenthetical title "(Tavazon)" corresponds to no Persian word I can identify and appears to be an artifact; delete it. And "خانه مهمان" is not idiomatic — Rumi's own word (and the word in the section heading) is the compound **مهمان‌خانه** (*mehmān-khāneh*, "guest-house"). "The Guest House" as a title is Coleman Barks's, not Rumi's; the passage has no title, only the prose rubrics of the critical editions.

## 1. Persian Text Accuracy

**The opening line does not match the received text.** The draft gives:

> این تن خانه مهمان است ای جوان

The text in the standard editions (confirmed against Ganjoor §155 and masnavi.net's line 3644, both reproducing the received critical text) is:

> **هست مهمان‌خانه این تن ای جوان**

Same meaning, different words — and the draft's version **breaks the meter**. The Masnavi is in *ramal-i musaddas-i maḥdhūf* (fāʿilātun fāʿilātun fāʿilun); "hast mehmān-khāne īn tan ay javān" scans, the draft's rewording does not. This looks like a back-translation into Persian from the English rather than a quotation of the edition. Since the publication claims to rest on Nicholson's text, this is the single most damaging error after the language misnomer.

**The eight couplets are a composite of two non-contiguous passages, and this is nowhere disclosed.**

- Couplets 1–3 = Masnavi **V.3644–3646**, section 155 ("تمثیل تن آدمی به مهمان‌خانه و اندیشه‌های مختلف به مهمانان مختلف…"). That section contains only these three couplets.
- Couplets 5–8 = Masnavi **V.3676–3679**, the opening of section 157 ("تمثیل فکر هر روزینه کی اندر دل آید به مهمان نو…").
- Between them sits all of section 156 — the *story* of the guest whom the householder's wife resents ("…مهمان در گردن ما ماند," the very idiom of line 3645) — which the compilation silently omits.
- Couplet 4 ("فکر در سینه در آید نو به نو / خند خندان پیش او تو باز رو") is genuine but occurs **later** in section 157, not between 3646 and 3676. Its relocation creates redundancy in the English (Verses 4 and 5 now say nearly the same thing back to back).

This splice-and-reorder is, almost exactly, the editorial move Barks made to create "The Guest House" — which strongly suggests the "reconstruction" was assembled to match the shape of the famous English poem rather than drawn from Nicholson. A translation whose selling point is "what Barks removed" cannot itself silently excise thirty lines and scramble the order. Fix: print the two passages separately with their real line numbers (V.3644–46; V.3676–80 ff.), state that the popular "poem" is a modern composite of them, and either include or explicitly summarize the intervening story.

Minor: several words have lost their zero-width joiners or been misdivided (میزند → می‌زند; سینهات → سینه‌ات; غیبِ وش → غیب‌وش, a single compound *ghayb-vash*, "unseen-like"). The Translation Notes quote a garbled line "این کین ماند اندر گردنم" that doubles the demonstrative — the text is "هین مگو **کین** ماند اندر گردنم," where کین = که این, "that this."

**Sourcing**: the attribution of masnavi.net's Persian text to a "Mahdi Azar Yazdi edition" is almost certainly wrong — Mehdi Azar Yazdi was a children's author (*Good Stories for Good Children*), not an editor of the Masnavi. I could not verify any such edition; before publication, collate directly against Nicholson's printed volumes (Books V–VI text volume, Gibb Memorial Series), not against aggregator websites. The description of Nicholson's edition itself (8 vols: 3 text, 3 translation, 2 commentary, 1925–1940) is correct.

## 2. Sufi Terminology

**Transliterations — three of five are wrong, one badly:**

| Draft | Correct | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ضیف "dhaif" | **ḍayf** (Ar.); Persian pronunciation *zeyf* | "dh" transliterates ذ/ﺫ, a different letter; "dhaif" also collides with ضعیف (*ḍaʿīf*, "weak") |
| عدم "adum" / "'adum" | **ʿadam** | no "u" in the word |
| خانه "khāneh" | fine | |
| فکر "fikr" | fine | |
| شادی "shādī" | fine | |

**Glosses — a pattern of over-reading plain words while missing the genuinely loaded one:**

- **ضیف**: an ordinary Arabic loanword for "guest." The idea that the guest is *sent* is supported — by line 3646 itself ("whatever comes **from the world of the unseen** into your heart is a guest"), and the section heading's invocation of Abraham (خلیل) honoring every guest, believer or infidel. But "it is qadr (divine decree)" imports a technical term that appears nowhere in the passage. Argue divine sending from 3646 and the Khalīl rubric, not from vocabulary the text doesn't use.
- **خانه**: not a Sufi technical term, and the gloss inverts the poem's own image: Rumi says the **body** (تن) is the guest-house and the guest lodges **in the heart** (در دلت, 3646). The draft's claim that khāneh "means the heart" erases a distinction Rumi is deliberately drawing.
- **عدم**: genuinely important in Rumi's metaphysics — forms continually arise from and return to nonexistence, which he sometimes calls the treasury or quarry of being (Chittick discusses this at length in *The Sufi Path of Love*). But **ʿadam is not fanā**. Fanā is the annihilation of the mystic's ego in God; here the subject that "flies back into nonexistence" is the *guest/thought*, not the seeker's self. Keep "non-existence," annotate with Rumi's ʿadam-metaphysics, delete the fanā equation.
- **فکر**: the most defensible gloss. Fikr is a real technical term (discursive reflection, conventionally paired with dhikr), and section 157's own rubric likens each day's thought to a new guest. "Divine instruction," though, is stronger than the text; "visitor from the unseen" is what the text licenses.
- **شادی / اصل خیر**: shādī is the ordinary Persian word for joy — not a term of art. See §6 on اصل خیر.
- **Missed**: the one word in these lines that *is* doing quiet Sufi work is **غیر** (*ghayr*, "other") in 3679 — "it sweeps the house of *ghayr*," resonant with ماسوی الله, "everything other than God." The draft's rendering ("emptying it clean") drops it entirely. This is the passage's real theological payload, and the annotation apparatus over-reads five plain words while missing it.

## 3. Persian Grammar and Semantics (the "literal" layer)

The word-by-word section covers only couplets 1–2, not the whole text as the review request implies. Within it:

- **باز پرد**: باز here is the adverb "back/again" — *bāz parrad*, "it will fly back (into nonexistence)." The gloss "باز = opens / returns" and the rendering "it **opens** and flies back" are wrong; there is no opening, and the poetic version's "unfolds its wings" is pure invention on top of the misparse.
- **ماند اندر گردنم**: correctly identified as idiomatic ("remains on my neck" = will be stuck with me as a burden). Nicely, the rubric of the omitted section 156 uses the very same idiom (مهمان در گردن ما ماند) — evidence worth citing if the story is restored.
- **Verse 6 inverts Rumi's instruction — the most serious translation error in the draft.** The Persian "فکر را ای جان به جای شخص دان" is a plain imperative: *dān* = "know/regard" — "**Regard thought, O soul, as a person**, for the person has worth and life from thought." The draft renders: "Take the thought as person? **No**, O soul—". There is no negation in the line; the English says the opposite of the Persian. Nicholson renders it along the lines of "O soul, regard thought as a person, since a person derives his worth from thought."
- **راه شادی می‌زند**: *rāh zadan* is "to waylay, to rob (a traveler)" — the highwayman idiom. "If the thought of grief **waylays** joy, it is (nevertheless) making preparations for joy." The draft glosses می‌زند as "strikes/makes/opens" and translates "make the way for joy" — backwards. And the correct reading is *stronger* for the draft's own thesis: Rumi's paradox is that sorrow appears as a robber blocking joy's road while secretly working in joy's service. The draft's "opens the path" flattens the very paradox its commentary celebrates. (The gloss تطهیر "purification" in the notes is likewise imported; the word is not in the text.)
- The reading of شخص as "literally face or form": Arabic *shakhṣ* originally means the visible figure/silhouette of a person — "form/figure" is defensible, "face" is not.
- The "inverts Western psychology" framing (self as product of thought) is anachronistic editorializing, but the underlying textual claim — a person's worth and life derive from fikr — is genuinely in line 3677 and in Nicholson. Keep the claim, cut the anachronism, and fix the inverted English first.

## 4. Islamic Theological Content

The direction of the argument is right: this passage is Islamic and Sufi through and through, and its popular renderings do strip that. The section heading names Abraham's hospitality (اکرام ضیف, echoing Qur'anic ḍayf Ibrāhīm); line 3646 grounds the guests in the ghayb (the unseen — a Qur'anic category); ʿadam and aṣl-i khayr carry real metaphysical weight; and a few lines past the draft's excerpt Rumi says outright that **"grief is the slave of the people of certainty"** (که بود غم بندهٔ اهل یقین, V.3684 area) — a line that supports the translator's thesis far better than any of the imported vocabulary.

But as written, the apparatus claims Rumi's text contains **qadr**, **tawhīd**, **fanā**, and **taṭhīr**. None of these words occurs in the passage. The theology is there; these particular technical terms are not, and asserting them as textual content is exactly the kind of liberty the project condemns in Barks — in the opposite direction. The fix is easy and strengthens the case: cite the real anchors (Khalīl rubric, ghayb, ʿadam, ghayr, aṣl-i khayr, and the ahl-i yaqīn line from the continuation) instead of overlaying absent terminology.

## 5. Translation Quality (English)

The verse is competent and generally closer to the Persian than Barks. Specific problems, in severity order:

1. Verse 6: inverted meaning (see §3). Must be fixed.
2. Verse 7: "make the way for joy" — mistranslates the waylaying idiom and loses the paradox.
3. Verse 2: "unfolds its wings toward non-being" — invented image on a misparsed باز; "will fly back into non-existence" is both accurate and better poetry.
4. Verse 8: "emptying it clean" — drops ز غیر, the theologically loaded word.
5. Verses 4–5: redundant with each other, an artifact of the scrambled couplet order; resolves itself once the text is re-sequenced.
6. Verse 5's "O friend" has no warrant in the Persian line.

## 6. The Three Flagged Passages

**Passage 1 (عدم, line 3645).** The translator's instinct to hesitate is correct. The surface sense is simply that the transient guest "will presently fly back into nonexistence" — Nicholson reads it this way. The mystical resonance is real but is Rumi's *ʿadam*-metaphysics (nonexistence as the reservoir from which forms come and to which they return), **not** fanā. Recommended: translate plainly ("flies back into non-existence"), and carry the metaphysics in a note citing Chittick on ʿadam. The fanā reading is not justified.

**Passage 2 (lines 3677–3678).** Half-right. That the person's worth and life derive from thought is genuinely in 3677 — the reading is faithful, and not a modern projection, though the "Western psychology" framing is. But 3678 does not say sorrow "opens the way": it says sorrow **waylays/robs** joy's road *and yet* works in joy's service. The paradox is sharper than the draft's version — keep the servant-of-joy conclusion, restore the robber image. (And fix the inverted imperative in the English of 3677 before anything else.)

**Passage 3 (اصل خیر, line 3679).** Lexically, *aṣl-i khayr* is "the root/origin of good" — no explicit divine name. In context — the house swept of *ghayr* so that new joy may enter from the origin of good — a divine referent is a reasonable *interpretive* gloss, and consonant with Rumi's theology of God as the source of all good. Present it as interpretation supported by the ghayr/khayr pairing, not as the word's meaning. As it happens, the strongest support was sitting unquoted in the same section: grief as "the slave of the people of certainty," acting on behalf of the Unseen's purposes.

## 7. Overall Verdict

**The thesis deserves publication; this draft does not, yet.** The claim that popular versions (Barks above all) de-Islamize Rumi is well founded and well documented in the scholarship the draft already cites — Lewis's *Rumi: Past and Present* discusses precisely this. And several of the draft's readings (fikr as guest, the body/heart guest-house distinction, sorrow in joy's service) are genuinely there in the Persian.

But in its current state the translation commits, in miniature, the sins it prosecutes: it silently splices non-contiguous passages into a "poem" Rumi never wrote as one; it prints a Persian line Rumi didn't write; it inserts a negation that reverses one line's meaning; and it overlays technical vocabulary (qadr, fanā, tawhīd) the text doesn't contain — while missing the loaded word (ghayr) it does. Add the "Middle Persian" misnomer and the broken transliterations, and a competent reviewer at any journal or press would reject it in the first two pages.

### Required before publication

1. Replace every instance of "Middle Persian" with "Classical Persian."
2. Correct line 3644 to هست مهمان‌خانه این تن ای جوان and collate all eight couplets against Nicholson's printed text (not aggregator sites).
3. Disclose the composite structure: V.3644–46 (§155) + V.3676–79 ff. (§157), with §156's story omitted or summarized; restore couplet order.
4. Fix Verse 6 (remove the inserted negation) and Verse 7 (waylay, not "make way").
5. Fix transliterations: ḍayf, ʿadam.
6. Re-scope the terminology notes: drop qadr/fanā/tawhīd/taṭhīr as textual claims; add ghayr; cite the ahl-i yaqīn line.
7. Remove "(Tavazon)"; retitle the Persian as مهمان‌خانه or drop the invented Persian title.
