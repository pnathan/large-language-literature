Tablet I

The Coming of Enkidu

The prologue praises the man who brought back knowledge from before the Flood, then commands the reader onto the wall of Uruk and into the tablet-box. Gilgameš oppresses his city; the gods answer with a counterweight. Aruru pinches clay, and Enkidu is born among the herds. A hunter's complaint sets Šamhat on the road; six days and seven nights make the wild man human. Two dreams — a meteor and an axe, each loved like a wife — announce the friend.

anagbuI 1

nagbu is both 'deep spring' and 'totality': he who saw the source, and he who saw everything. The translation keeps the water and lets the pun go.

b[alkakāti īd]ûI 2

The verb is secure; the noun is restored. Older editions read ša kullat īdû here; the eBL text prints [alkakāti], 'the ways.'

csimmiltuI 15

'Staircase,' not 'threshold' as older translations render: the ascent to Eanna is architectural and processional.

dšārI 22–23

One šār = 3,600, a beloved round number; here a unit of urban area. The prologue ends in a surveyor's docket — deliberately.

ailitti qūltiI 102

'Offspring of stillness' — or of the midnight hush, or of Silence personified. Uncertain and untranslatable; the alternative reading takes it as 'offspring of the skull/of Anu.' The strangeness is original.

bkīma nissabaI 105

Literally 'like Nissaba,' goddess of grain: his hair grows like a barley-field. So too line 58.

≈ṭīda iktariṣ — the clay ring

Aruru pinches off ṭiṭṭu, clay, and Enkidu exists. The word closes every circle the poem opens: Enkidu “turned to clay” (X 68), the drowned world “gone back to clay” (XI 135), Ištar wishing the fatal day itself “had turned back to clay” (XI 119). Creation and annihilation are one substance; English “clay” keeps the word but not the ring.

≈kiṣir Ninurta ≈ kiṣru ša Ānu

Enkidu is kiṣir Ninurta, a “knot” of Ninurta’s strength; the dream-meteor of line 246 is kiṣru ša Ānu, a “lump” of Anu. One noun, kiṣru — knot, clot, concentration — binds the omen to the man before any interpreter speaks: the dream is solved lexically. See the note at 246.

aI 192I 192

Six days and seven nights: the same span as Ūta-napišti's test of sleep (XI 209) and the mourning over Enkidu (X 235). The poem counts in sevens.

bSeven nights, or six — OB II 48

The Old Babylonian Pennsylvania tablet counts seven days and seven nights with Šamhat; the late edition trimmed the count to six-and-seven, aligning it with its other sevens — the mourning vigil (X 235) and the test of sleep (XI 209).

≈Šamhat ≈ šamhu

Her name is the common adjective šamhu, “lush, luxuriant, magnificent” — a professional name that is also Uruk’s own epithet-family. In the OB Pennsylvania tablet the dreaming king says šamhākū-ma, “I walked in my luxuriance” (OB II 4–5): he struts in her name a column before he hears it. The English “Šamhat” is mute; the Akkadian ear hears “Lady Lush.”

akiṣru ša ĀniI 246

A 'lump of Anu' — meteoric iron falling from the sky-god's realm; the same phrase measures Enkidu's strength at I 123 and elsewhere. One noun binds omen to man.

≈kiṣru ša Ānu ≈ kiṣir Ninurta

The falling “lump of Anu” repeats the noun that made Enkidu at 102 — and the fixed simile of his strength (“hard as a lump of the sky-rock of Anu,” 123, 135, 150 …) keeps the equation sounding through the whole tablet. The friend is the meteor; the omen was never obscure.

The two recensions part

The dream of the meteor

Same dream, five centuries apart. The old telling is brisk and bodily — iktabit elīya, “it was too heavy for me”; the late telling reads dān elīya, “too strong for me,” and adds the gathered land, the kissed feet, and the mother’s full oracle. Weight became strength under the redactor’s hand.
