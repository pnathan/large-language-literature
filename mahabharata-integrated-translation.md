## INTRODUCTION: WHAT YOU ARE ABOUT TO ENCOUNTER

The Mahābhārata is the longest poem in the world, and its central catastrophe takes place not on a battlefield but in a gambling hall. A king who is called "the Dharma King" — the son of the god Dharma himself — stakes and loses his wealth, his kingdom, his four brothers, his own person, and finally his wife. His wife is dragged into the assembly by her hair, menstruating, wearing a single garment, and a prince attempts to strip her naked before the assembled elders of the dynasty. Her five husbands, the greatest warriors alive, sit and watch. The grandfather of the dynasty, the wisest man in the hall, is asked directly whether this is lawful, and answers that he cannot say. Then the whole sequence happens *a second time*, and the king who lost everything accepts the second invitation too — telling the messenger, in so many words, that he knows it will destroy him.

Read with Western assumptions, this is incomprehensible. The good men look like cowards. The wise men look like moral failures. The king looks like a fool. And the war that follows — eighteen armies annihilated in eighteen days to answer what happened in that hall — looks like revenge on a genocidal scale.

None of those readings is available to the text itself. The Mahābhārata is built on a concept, **dharma**, for which English has no word, and the entire catastrophe of the dicing hall is engineered — by the poem, deliberately — out of dharma's own logic. This edition exists to install that concept in you *before* you meet the narrative, using only the poem's own words.

### The word that will not translate

*Dharma* comes from the Sanskrit root **√dhṛ**, "to hold, to bear, to support." The Śānti Parva states the etymology directly: *dhāraṇād dharma ity āhur dharmo dhārayate prajāḥ* — "they call it dharma from upholding; dharma upholds the creatures" (12.110.11). Dharma is not "duty," not "morality," not "religion," not "law," though translators have used all four. It is *that which holds things up* — the load-bearing structure of the cosmos, of society, of a family, of a single life. When you meet the word in this edition it will be left untranslated, because every English substitute imports a Western frame the text does not share:

- **"Duty"** implies an obligation you could, in principle, decline. Dharma cannot be declined; it can only be upheld or violated, and violation has structural, not merely moral, consequences.
- **"Morality"** implies universal rules applying identically to everyone. Dharma is *indexed*: a priest's dharma, a warrior's dharma, a wife's dharma, a slave's dharma differ, and may contradict one another.
- **"Law"** implies a legislator and an enforcer. Dharma has neither. It is closer to load-bearing architecture: remove a supporting wall and no judge condemns you — the roof simply comes down, on the guilty and innocent alike.

Four layers of dharma operate in the passages ahead, and you should learn to see them separately: **universal dharma** (truth, non-cruelty, protection of the helpless — binding on all beings); **class dharma** (*varṇa-dharma* — what a warrior, a priest, a king must do *because of what he is*); **personal dharma** (*svadharma* — the specific obligations of one's own station, vows, and moment); and **crisis dharma** (*āpad-dharma* — what happens when the layers collide). The Mahābhārata's tragedy lives entirely in the fourth layer. Its horror is not that people fail to do what is right; it is that the layers of rightness contradict each other, and the text knows it, and says so.

### Four companion terms

**Ṛta.** The older Vedic word for cosmic order — the regularity by which seasons turn and rivers run. By the epic's period *ṛta* as a noun has largely retired in favor of dharma, but it survives inside the epic's vocabulary in two fossils you will meet in these very passages: *ṛtu*, "season" (the ordered turning of the year, 1.1.37), and *anṛta*, "untruth" — literally the *un-ṛta*, that which runs against the order (2.61.56–57). When Vidura warns the silent assembly that they are incurring the fruit of *anṛta*, the old cosmology is speaking: a lie is not a misdeed but a *disorder*.

**Karma.** Simply "act, deed" — but in this cosmology every deed is load-bearing. Acts do not evaporate; they accumulate consequence with the impersonality of weight added to a scale, across lifetimes if necessary. No one presides over this. It is bookkeeping without a bookkeeper.

**Daiva and the Dhātṛ.** *Daiva* — "the ordained," fate, what comes from beyond human agency; the *Dhātṛ* is "the Placer," the arranging power that distributes good and evil. When Yudhiṣṭhira says (2.52.18) that a man follows the Placer's control "as if bound with nooses," he is not making an excuse. He is stating the physics of his world.

**Tapas** ("heat"). Austerity as accumulated power: renunciation, in this cosmology, is not self-denial but energy storage — heat built up by discipline until it can curse kings and compel gods. It explains why forest hermits are dangerous and why one hermit boy's curse, in our third passage, can kill an emperor.

**Avatāra / aṃśāvataraṇa** ("descent" / "descent by portions"). Divine beings take birth in human wombs — not as metaphor but as mechanism. Our fourth passage shows why this matters catastrophically: the heroes of this story are gods incarnated *for a purpose fixed before their births*.

### How to read this edition

Twelve passages follow, in three movements. **Part I** (Ādi Parva, Book 1) builds the cosmology: cyclical time, dharma as reciprocal upholding, and the divine descent that pre-loads the war. **Part II** (Sabhā Parva, Book 2) shows the trap being built out of dharma itself. **Part III** shows the trap closing: the stake, the question, the disrobing, the paralysis, and the second game. Each passage is given in IAST-romanized Sanskrit from the BORI critical edition, then in a *literal* translation (as close to word-order and syntax as English tolerates), then in a *fresh rendering* (readable English preserving the alien logic), then an annotation. Transitions carry the argument between passages.

One warning. Do not read for heroes and villains. Read for **load paths** — for where the weight of the cosmos is being carried, and what happens when a supporting member is struck.

## PART I — THE COSMIC FRAME (Ādi Parva)

### Passage 1: The Egg and the Wheel
**Mahābhārata 1.1.27–29, 36–38**

> niṣprabhe 'smin nirāloke sarvatas tamasāvṛte
> bṛhad aṇḍam abhūd ekaṃ prajānāṃ bījam akṣayam
> yugasyādau nimittaṃ tan mahad divyaṃ pracakṣate
> yasmiṃs tac chrūyate satyaṃ jyotir brahma sanātanam
> adbhutaṃ cāpy acintyaṃ ca sarvatra samatāṃ gatam
> avyaktaṃ kāraṇaṃ sūkṣmaṃ yat tat sadasadātmakam
> …
> yad idaṃ dṛśyate kiṃ cid bhūtaṃ sthāvarajaṅgamam
> punaḥ saṃkṣipyate sarvaṃ jagat prāpte yugakṣaye
> yathartāv ṛtuliṅgāni nānārūpāṇi paryaye
> dṛśyante tāni tāny eva tathā bhāvā yugādiṣu
> evam etad anādyantaṃ bhūtasaṃhārakārakam
> anādinidhanaṃ loke cakraṃ saṃparivartate

**Literal.** When this [world] was without radiance, without light, covered on all sides by darkness, there came to be one great egg, the inexhaustible seed of creatures. They declare it the great divine cause at the beginning of the yuga; in it, we hear, was the true light, the eternal *brahman* — wondrous, unthinkable, gone equally into everything, the unmanifest subtle cause, that whose nature is both the existent and the non-existent. … Whatever is seen here — any being, standing still or moving — the entire world is compressed together again when the destruction of the yuga arrives. As in a season [*ṛtu*] the marks of the season appear, various in form, at each turning — those very same ones — so do the states of being at each beginning of a yuga. Thus this wheel, without beginning or end, maker of the destruction of beings, beginningless and deathless, revolves in the world.

**Rendering.** Before light, there was one thing in the dark: an egg, the seed of everything that would ever live, and inside it the eternal brightness that is both what is and what is not. And everything you now see — everything that stands and everything that moves — will be folded back into compression when this age burns out, and unfolded again, the same things returning the way the marks of a season return: not similar things, *the same things*. A wheel with no rim-joint, no starting point, no maker and no mercy, turns through the world, and its turning is the destruction and remaking of all beings.

**Annotation.** The epic's very first cosmological statement abolishes two Western defaults at once. First, *time is a circle, not an arrow*: the story you are about to read has, in the deepest sense, already happened, and will happen again. Second, note the simile the poet reaches for: the yugas return "as the marks of a season (*ṛtu*) return." *Ṛtu* is the living cognate of the old Vedic *ṛta*, cosmic order — the universe is regular the way a year is regular. Nobody enforces spring. Nobody will enforce the coming catastrophe either. The word *yugakṣaya*, "the wearing-out of the age," should be kept in mind: the dicing hall and the war belong to the exhaustion phase of a cosmic cycle, the point in the wheel's turn where the structure is due to come down.

*Transition.* A wheel that turns without a turner raises the question: what holds the world's shape between dissolutions? The epic's answer is dharma — and it makes an audacious claim about its own relationship to that answer.

### Passage 2: The Totality Claim
**Mahābhārata 1.56.33**

> dharme cārthe ca kāme ca mokṣe ca bharatarṣabha
> yad ihāsti tad anyatra yan nehāsti na tat kva cit

**Literal.** In dharma and in wealth and in desire and in liberation, O bull of the Bharatas: what is here is elsewhere; what is not here is nowhere at all.

**Rendering.** Concerning dharma, concerning profit, concerning pleasure, concerning release: whatever exists, exists in this poem. What this poem does not contain does not exist.

**Annotation.** This is the most famous verse of the Ādi Parva, and it defines the epic's genre — which is not "epic" in the Homeric sense at all. The Mahābhārata presents itself as a *complete inventory of the four aims of human existence*, with dharma listed first. The verse instructs you how to read everything that follows: the dice game is not an episode in an adventure story; it is a *demonstration* inside an encyclopedia of dharma. When the text shows you righteous men paralyzed and a queen assaulted in open court, it is not failing to deliver a moral; it is delivering the most advanced lesson it has — that dharma is real the way gravity is real, and just as indifferent to whether you are crushed.

*Transition.* But what kind of thing is this dharma, that a poem could inventory it? The epic's first working definition arrives, characteristically, not as doctrine but as a father correcting his son — in a story about a curse that will kill a blameless king.

### Passage 3: "Dharma, Struck Down, Strikes Down"
**Mahābhārata 1.37.20–24** — *The sage Śamīka rebukes his son Śṛṅgin, who has cursed King Parikṣit to die for a trivial insult.*

> na me priyaṃ kṛtaṃ tāta naiṣa dharmas tapasvinām …
> vayaṃ tasya narendrasya viṣaye nivasāmahe
> nyāyato rakṣitās tena tasya pāpaṃ na rocaye
> sarvathā vartamānasya rājño hy asmadvidhaiḥ sadā
> kṣantavyaṃ putra dharmo hi hato hanti na saṃśayaḥ
> yadi rājā na rakṣeta pīḍā vai naḥ parā bhavet
> na śaknuyāma carituṃ dharmaṃ putra yathāsukham
> rakṣyamāṇā vayaṃ tāta rājabhiḥ śāstradṛṣṭibhiḥ
> carāmo vipulaṃ dharmaṃ teṣāṃ cāṃśo 'sti dharmataḥ

**Literal.** You have not done what pleases me, child; this is not the dharma of ascetics. … We dwell in the realm of that lord of men; we are protected by him according to right order; I do not approve evil toward him. A king conducting himself in whatever way must always be forgiven by such as us, son — for dharma, struck down, strikes down: no doubt. If the king did not protect, extreme affliction would be ours; we could not practice dharma at our ease, son. Protected by kings whose eyes are the treatises, we practice abundant dharma — and of that dharma, by dharma, a share is theirs.

**Rendering.** "What you have done gives me no joy, child. This is not how ascetics uphold. We live inside that king's protection; struck at him, I will not approve the blow. Whatever a king does, men like us must forgive him — because dharma that is struck down strikes down the striker. Of this there is no doubt. If the king did not protect, we could not afford our holiness. Guarded by kings who see through the treatises' eyes, we practice vast dharma — and a share of everything we practice flows back, by dharma, to them."

**Annotation.** Here is dharma's *mechanism*, stated in six words: **dharmo hi hato hanti** — "dharma, struck, strikes." Not "God punishes," not "justice is served." The syntax is that of a physical recoil: the violated structure itself does the violence, automatically, "no doubt" (*na saṃśayaḥ*). Notice, too, the economy of upholding the passage describes: the king's protection *enables* the hermits' dharma, and a *share* (*aṃśa*) of their practice accrues to him — dharma circulates like credit through the social body. Everyone is holding up everyone. This is why, in the dicing hall, an assault on one woman will be felt as a structural strike against the whole edifice — and why the recoil, when it comes, will take down the whole edifice's defenders and assailants alike. (The boy's curse, by the way, stands: Parikṣit dies of it. In this cosmology even a wrongly-thrown curse is a deed, and deeds do not come back.)

*Transition.* One scale of that recoil remains to be shown — the largest one. Why is *this* story, of *these* cousins, the story in which dharma's recoil operates at world scale? Because, the epic tells us, the war was arranged in heaven before any of its fighters were born.

### Passage 4: The Earth Cannot Bear Herself
**Mahābhārata 1.58.29, 36–37, 44, 46, 47e** — *The* aṃśāvataraṇa*, "the descent by portions."*

> jātair iha mahīpāla jāyamānaiś ca tair mahī
> na śaśākātmanātmānam iyaṃ dhārayituṃ dharā
> …
> na hīmāṃ pavano rājan na nāgā na nagā mahīm
> tadā dhārayituṃ śekur ākrāntāṃ dānavair balāt
> tato mahī mahīpāla bhārārtā bhayapīḍitā
> jagāma śaraṇaṃ devaṃ sarvabhūtapitāmaham
> …
> yadartham asi saṃprāptā matsakāśaṃ vasuṃdhare
> tadarthaṃ saṃniyokṣyāmi sarvān eva divaukasaḥ
> …
> asyā bhūmer nirasituṃ bhāraṃ bhāgaiḥ pṛthak pṛthak
> asyām eva prasūyadhvaṃ virodhāyeti cābravīt
> …
> svair aṃśaiḥ saṃprasūyadhvaṃ yatheṣṭaṃ mānuṣeṣv iti

**Literal.** With those [demons] born here and being born, O protector of the earth, this earth — the Bearer (*dharā*) — was not able by herself to bear (*dhārayitum*) herself. … Neither wind, O king, nor serpents nor mountains could then bear this earth, overrun by the Dānavas by force. Then the earth, O protector of the earth, pained by the burden, oppressed by fear, went for refuge to the god who is grandfather of all beings. … [Brahmā:] "For the purpose for which you have come into my presence, Earth-bearer-of-treasure, for that purpose I will commission all the dwellers in heaven." … "To cast off this earth's burden, each by his separate portion, be born upon her — *for conflict*," he said. … "By your own portions be born, as you please, among humans."

**Rendering.** So many demons had poured into birth that the earth — she whose very name means *the one who bears* — could no longer bear her own weight. Wind could not hold her up; the world-serpents could not; the mountains could not. Crushed under the load and sick with fear, she went to the Grandfather of all beings and asked for refuge. And Brahmā said: "For this I will conscript all of heaven. Shed your burden. Gods — be born on her, each by a portion of yourself, *for the sake of the collision*. Take birth among humans as you please."

**Annotation.** Read the puns first, because they are the theology. The earth is *dharā*, "the bearer," and what fails is her power *dhārayitum*, "to bear" — the same root √dhṛ that gives *dharma*. When adharma multiplies, the text does not say the earth becomes wicked; it says she becomes **too heavy**. Overload, not sin. And the remedy Brahmā decrees is the mechanism called *aṃśāvataraṇa*: gods descending "by portions" (*aṃśa*) into human wombs — explicitly **virodhāya, "for conflict."** The war at the end of the Mahābhārata is not an outcome that better choices might have averted. It is the *purpose for which its participants were born*: a controlled demolition, scheduled in heaven, to relieve a structural overload. Yudhiṣṭhira is the god Dharma's portion; Draupadī is born from a fire-altar; Kṛṣṇa is the supreme god's descent. Keep this passage in mind at every moment of the dicing scenes — every character who "fails" to stop the catastrophe is, at the cosmic register, a load-bearing member of the catastrophe. The tragedy will be that they do not feel like instruments. They feel everything.

*Transition.* The frame is now complete: circular time in its exhaustion phase, dharma as automatic recoil, a world officially overweight, and gods born into flesh to bring the weight down. Now watch the poem build the demolition's trigger — out of dharma itself.

## PART II — THE TRAP IS BUILT (Sabhā Parva)

### Passage 5: The Attack Surface
**Mahābhārata 2.44.18–19; 2.45.38** — *Duryodhana, sick with envy at Yudhiṣṭhira's imperial glory, asks his uncle Śakuni how such a man can be beaten.*

> dyūtapriyaś ca kaunteyo na ca jānāti devitum
> samāhūtaś ca rājendro na śakṣyati nivartitum
> devane kuśalaś cāhaṃ na me 'sti sadṛśo bhuvi
> triṣu lokeṣu kaunteyaṃ taṃ tvaṃ dyūte samāhvaya
> …
> āhūtaś caiṣyati vyaktaṃ dīvyāvety āhvayasva tam

**Literal.** The son of Kuntī is fond of dicing, and he does not know how to play; and summoned, the king of kings will not be able to turn back. And I am skilled in play; there is not my equal on earth — in the three worlds. Challenge that son of Kuntī to a game. … Summoned, he will come — it is certain. "Let us two play" — challenge him thus.

**Rendering.** "Yudhiṣṭhira loves the dice and cannot play them. And — this is the point — once formally summoned, that king *cannot turn back*. I, meanwhile, have no equal at the dice in the three worlds. So summon him. He will come. It is certain. Say only: *let us play.*"

**Annotation.** Śakuni's plan is three sentences long, and the load-bearing sentence is the second: *samāhūtaś ca … na śakṣyati nivartitum* — "and summoned, he will not be able to turn back." Not "will not want to"; **will not be able to** (*na śakṣyati*). Śakuni has identified that a kṣatriya of Yudhiṣṭhira's caliber is *programmable*: the warrior-dharma that forbids refusing a formal challenge — the same reflex that makes him invincible in war — can be invoked by an enemy at will. This is the alien pivot of the whole epic: the villains' weapon is not the hero's weakness but **the hero's dharma**. A Western Iago exploits jealousy, ambition, appetite — flaws. Śakuni exploits integrity. The better a man Yudhiṣṭhira is, the more certainly the trap closes. Note also the shape of the certainty: *vyaktam*, "it is manifest/certain." In a clockwork cosmos, virtue makes you predictable.

*Transition.* Śakuni's theory now meets its test. The summons is delivered; Vidura himself, the incarnation of Dharma's wisdom, is forced to carry it. Here is Yudhiṣṭhira receiving it — and explaining, with complete clarity, why he will walk into the trap he can see.

### Passage 6: "Challenged, I Do Not Turn Back"
**Mahābhārata 2.52.16, 18, 21** — *Yudhiṣṭhira to Vidura, and the narrator, as the Pāṇḍavas set out for Hāstinapura.*

> na cākāmaḥ śakuninā devitāhaṃ
> na cen māṃ dhṛṣṇur āhvayitā sabhāyām
> āhūto 'haṃ na nivarte kadā cit
> tad āhitaṃ śāśvataṃ vai vrataṃ me
> …
> daivaṃ prajñāṃ tu muṣṇāti tejaś cakṣur ivāpatat
> dhātuś ca vaśam anveti pāśair iva naraḥ sitaḥ
> …
> dhṛtarāṣṭreṇa cāhūtaḥ kālasya samayena ca

**Literal.** And I would not play with Śakuni unwilling — if the bold one did not challenge me in the assembly. [But] challenged, I never turn back: that is my established, everlasting vow. … Daiva steals the understanding, as a glare falling on the eye [steals sight]; and a man follows the control of the Placer as if bound with nooses. … Summoned by Dhṛtarāṣṭra — and by the appointment of Time.

**Rendering.** "I have no wish to play Śakuni. If he does not fling the challenge at me in open court, I will not play. But if I am challenged — I do not turn back. Ever. That vow was laid down in me and it does not expire." … Fate steals the mind the way glare strikes the eye; a man walks in the Placer's control like a beast led on nooses. … So he went: summoned by Dhṛtarāṣṭra — and by the appointed hour of Time.

**Annotation.** Everything the Western reader needs is in these three fragments, and everything they assume is contradicted. (1) Yudhiṣṭhira *sees the trap*. He names Śakuni; he knows the dicing will be crooked (a few verses later the narrator says he entered the second game "though he knew Śakuni's māyā"). (2) His compliance is not stupidity but a *vrata* — a vow, a piece of personal dharma he has installed in himself, "everlasting" (*śāśvatam*). In this cosmology, a vow is not a promise you keep; it is a structure you have become. Breaking it would not be prudence; it would be self-demolition of the very integrity that makes him the Dharma King. (3) The verse about *daiva* supplies the physics: fate does not overpower a man's mind from outside; it *blinds like glare* and *leads like nooses* — the imagery is of an intact will operating inside a channel it cannot see the walls of. And (4) the narrator's closing line quietly doubles the summons: called by Dhṛtarāṣṭra, *and by Time*. Remember Passage 4 — the demolition is scheduled. Two invitations arrive in one envelope, and only one of them is from a king.

*Transition.* One man in the epic sees the whole causal chain in advance and says it aloud. His warning is preserved precisely so that no reader can mistake what follows for accident.

### Passage 7: The Seer Names the Mechanism
**Mahābhārata 2.56.1, 3–4** — *Vidura to King Dhṛtarāṣṭra, begging him to stop the game before it begins.*

> dyūtaṃ mūlaṃ kalahasyānupāti
> mithobhedāya mahate vā raṇāya
> yad āsthito 'yaṃ dhṛtarāṣṭrasya putro
> duryodhanaḥ sṛjate vairam ugram
> …
> duryodhano madenaiva kṣemaṃ rāṣṭrād apohati
> viṣāṇaṃ gaur iva madāt svayam ārujate balāt
> yaś cittam anveti parasya rājan
> vīraḥ kaviḥ svām atipatya dṛṣṭim
> nāvaṃ samudra iva bālanetrām
> āruhya ghore vyasane nimajjet

**Literal.** Dicing is the root of quarrel, following after — toward mutual rupture, or toward great war. Taking his stand on it, this son of Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Duryodhana, is creating a terrible enmity. … Duryodhana out of sheer intoxication is thrusting security away from the kingdom, as a bull in rut breaks off his own horn by force. And whoever follows another's mind, O king — though hero, though sage — overriding his own sight, would board a boat steered by a child on the sea, and sink in horrible calamity.

**Rendering.** "Dicing is the root from which quarrel grows — and it grows toward rupture, or toward great war. By staging this game your son is manufacturing a hatred with a long life. Drunk on himself, he is prying the kingdom loose from its own safety, like a rutting bull snapping off his own horn. And a king who follows another's mind against his own eyes — hero or sage, no matter — has boarded a boat with a child at the helm, on open ocean, in weather."

**Annotation.** Vidura is dharma's clear sight in the poem (he is, in fact, another incarnate portion of the god Dharma), and his function here is diagnostic: *dyūtaṃ mūlam* — "dicing is the root," and the fruit is *mahate raṇāya*, "great war." The chain of consequence that will consume eighteen armies is stated, in court, before the first throw. And he is ignored — not because anyone disputes him, but because Dhṛtarāṣṭra's love for his son is *also* a dharma (a father's), and the blind king lets the weaker load-path carry the structure. Note what this passage does to the Western category of "tragic surprise": there is none. In a dharmic cosmos the future is not hidden; it is *announced*, the way an engineer announces that a beam is undersized. The catastrophe proceeds anyway, because announcement is not reinforcement. Knowledge, in this poem, saves no one — a theme that will reach its unbearable form when Yudhiṣṭhira accepts the second game "though he knew."

*Transition.* The game is played. Śakuni's dice do what crooked dice do. Wealth, kingdom, brothers, self — all staked, all lost, in a cascade the text delivers with liturgical repetition ("jito 'smi" — "I am won"). Then Śakuni's voice softens, and the trap shows its true floor.

### Passage 8: The Last Stake
**Mahābhārata 2.58.31–33, 35, 37–38** — *Yudhiṣṭhira has staked and lost himself. Śakuni offers one more throw.*

> [śakuniḥ:] asti vai te priyā devī glaha eko 'parājitaḥ
> paṇasva kṛṣṇāṃ pāñcālīṃ tayātmānaṃ punar jaya
> [yudhiṣṭhiraḥ:] naiva hrasvā na mahatī nātikṛṣṇā na rohiṇī
> sarāgaraktanetrā ca tayā dīvyāmy ahaṃ tvayā
> śāradotpalapatrākṣyā śāradotpalagandhayā …
> caramaṃ saṃviśati yā prathamaṃ pratibudhyate
> ā gopālāvipālebhyaḥ sarvaṃ veda kṛtākṛtam …
> tayaivaṃvidhayā rājan pāñcālyāhaṃ sumadhyayā
> glahaṃ dīvyāmi cārvaṅgyā draupadyā hanta saubala
> [vaiśaṃpāyanaḥ:] evam ukte tu vacane dharmarājena bhārata
> dhig dhig ity eva vṛddhānāṃ sabhyānāṃ niḥsṛtā giraḥ

**Literal.** [Śakuni:] There is indeed your beloved queen, one stake not yet lost. Wager Kṛṣṇā of Pāñcāla; by her win yourself back. [Yudhiṣṭhira:] Neither short is she nor tall, not too dark nor ruddy, her eyes reddened with passion — with her I play against you. With eyes like autumn-lotus petals, with the fragrance of the autumn lotus … she who lies down last and wakes first, who down to the cowherds and shepherds knows all that is done and undone … With her, of such a kind, O king — with the slender-waisted lady of Pāñcāla, with lovely-limbed Draupadī — I play a stake. Alas, son of Subala! [Narrator:] When this speech was spoken by the Dharma King, O Bhārata, "Shame! Shame!" — such were the cries that broke from the elders in the assembly.

**Rendering.** Śakuni: "One treasure is still unlost — your queen. Stake her, and win yourself back with her." And Yudhiṣṭhira — the Dharma King — began to praise what he was about to wager: "Not too short and not too tall, not too dark and not too fair, her eyes red with feeling — with *her* I play. Eyes like petals of the autumn lotus; scent of the autumn lotus. Last to bed and first to rise, who knows what is done and left undone down to the herdboys of the household. With her — so made, slender-waisted, lovely-limbed — with Draupadī, I cast this stake. *Alas, son of Subala.*" And out of the elders in that hall broke a single sound: *Shame. Shame.*

**Annotation.** This is deliberately the hardest passage in the edition, and no annotation should soften it. Two details are essential. First, Śakuni's hook is *restorative*: stake her *to win yourself back* — the offer is dressed as Yudhiṣṭhira's dharmic exit, a way to undo the abomination (which even Śakuni names, 2.58.29) of a king who wagered his own person. Every step deeper into the pit is presented as a step out of it. Second, the love-poetry of the stake: Yudhiṣṭhira describes Draupadī in the cadences of a bridegroom, ending with *hanta* — "alas." The text refuses to let him be numb; he knows exactly what she is worth, which is what makes staking her a cosmic strike rather than a clerical loss. And mark the assembly's *dhik dhik* — "Shame!" The hall *knows*, instantly and unanimously, that universal dharma has been violated. What the hall does with that knowledge — nothing — is the subject of the next movement. The question of whether a man who had already lost himself *could* legally stake a wife at all is about to be asked, and it will break the wisest head in the kingdom.

*Transition.* Draupadī is in the inner apartments, in a single garment, menstruating and therefore secluded — a state in which dharma itself forbids her appearing before men. A messenger arrives to tell her she has been gambled away. Her response is not a scream. It is a question of law that no one on earth will prove able to answer.

## PART III — THE CLOSING OF THE TRAP (Sabhā Parva)

### Passage 9: The Question
**Mahābhārata 2.60.7, 9, 13, 15; 2.60.40–41** — *Draupadī to the messenger; the narrator; Draupadī's words on being summoned; and Bhīṣma's answer in the hall.*

> [draupadī:] gaccha tvaṃ kitavaṃ gatvā sabhāyāṃ pṛccha sūtaja
> kiṃ nu pūrvaṃ parājaiṣīr ātmānaṃ māṃ nu bhārata
> [vaiśaṃpāyanaḥ:] yudhiṣṭhiras tu niśceṣṭo gatasattva ivābhavat
> na taṃ sūtaṃ pratyuvāca vacanaṃ sādhv asādhu vā
> [draupadī:] evaṃ nūnaṃ vyadadhāt saṃvidhātā
> sparśāv ubhau spṛśato dhīrabālau
> dharmaṃ tv ekaṃ paramaṃ prāha loke
> sa naḥ śamaṃ dhāsyati gopyamānaḥ
> [vaiśaṃpāyanaḥ:] ekavastrā adhonīvī rodamānā rajasvalā
> sabhām āgamya pāñcālī śvaśurasyāgrato 'bhavat
> …
> [bhīṣmaḥ:] na dharmasaukṣmyāt subhage vivaktuṃ
> śaknomi te praśnam imaṃ yathāvat
> asvo hy aśaktaḥ paṇituṃ parasvaṃ
> striyaś ca bhartur vaśatāṃ samīkṣya
> tyajeta sarvāṃ pṛthivīṃ samṛddhāṃ
> yudhiṣṭhiraḥ satyam atho na jahyāt
> uktaṃ jito 'smīti ca pāṇḍavena
> tasmān na śaknomi vivektum etat

**Literal.** [Draupadī:] Go, and having gone to the gambler in the assembly, ask, son of a sūta: which did you lose first — yourself, or me, O Bhārata? [Narrator:] But Yudhiṣṭhira was motionless, as if his life-essence had gone; he answered that messenger no word, good or bad. [Draupadī:] Thus surely the Disposer ordained it: touch strikes both the wise man and the child. He declared one thing supreme in the world — dharma; guarded, it will confer peace upon us. [Narrator:] In one garment, its knot below, weeping, in her courses, the lady of Pāñcāla came to the assembly and stood before her father-in-law. … [Bhīṣma:] From the subtlety of dharma, fair one, I am not able to resolve this your question rightly: one without property is powerless to stake another's property — yet considering that wives are in the husband's control… Yudhiṣṭhira would abandon the whole earth in her prosperity, but truth he would not abandon; and the Pāṇḍava said "I am won." Therefore I cannot untangle this.

**Rendering.** She did not weep first; she cross-examined. "Go back to the hall and ask the gambler this: *which did you lose first — yourself, or me?*" And in the hall, the Dharma King sat motionless, like a body the life has left, and answered nothing — not good, not bad. She said: "So the Disposer has disposed: what touches, touches sage and child alike. But there is one supreme thing in this world, and it is dharma — and if it is guarded, it will keep the peace over us." Then, in her single cloth, weeping, bleeding, she came into the assembly of kings and stood before the old men of the house. And Bhīṣma — the grandfather, the one man whose word could have ended it — said: "Because dharma is subtle, I cannot rightly resolve your question. A man who owns nothing cannot stake what is another's — but a wife is her husband's to command. Yudhiṣṭhira would sooner renounce the prosperous earth than his truth — and he said, *I am won*. Therefore I cannot untangle it."

**Annotation.** Draupadī's question — *kiṃ nu pūrvaṃ parājaiṣīḥ*, "which did you lose *first*?" — is the sharpest jurisprudential move in the epic. If Yudhiṣṭhira lost himself first, he was a slave with no property when he staked her; the stake is void; she is free. She has found, under assault, the exact seam in dharma's masonry. And the epic's answer to her, delivered through its most authoritative voice, is: **dharmasaukṣmyāt — "because of dharma's subtlety" — the question cannot be resolved.** This is the moment to discard, permanently, the idea that dharma is a rulebook. A rulebook returns answers. Dharma here behaves like a physical field near a singularity: real, binding, and *incomputable* at exactly the point where a human being most needs it computed. Note also what silences each man. Yudhiṣṭhira is not callous; he is *niśceṣṭaḥ*, motionless "as if the life had gone out of him" — a slave by his own truthful word, doubly forbidden to speak. Bhīṣma is not indifferent; he is honest — and his honesty ("I cannot untangle this") is worth nothing to the woman standing in one garment before him. In a Western courtroom, an unresolvable question halts proceedings. In this hall, unresolvability *permits* proceedings. That is the horror the poem has been building toward.

*Transition.* Into the vacuum left by the unanswered question steps Karṇa — the Pāṇḍavas' unacknowledged elder brother, Draupadī's rejected suitor — with a ruling of his own. His logic is impeccable, and monstrous, and it licenses the act the epic can barely bring itself to narrate.

### Passage 10: The Disrobing and the Wound in Dharma
**Mahābhārata 2.61.38, 40–42, 52, 56–57** — *Karṇa's order; the act; the marvel; Vidura's verdict on the hall.*

> [karṇaḥ:] duḥśāsana subālo 'yaṃ vikarṇaḥ prājñavādikaḥ
> pāṇḍavānāṃ ca vāsāṃsi draupadyāś cāpy upāhara
> …
> tato duḥśāsano rājan draupadyā vasanaṃ balāt
> sabhāmadhye samākṣipya vyapakraṣṭuṃ pracakrame
> ākṛṣyamāṇe vasane draupadyās tu viśāṃ pate
> tadrūpam aparaṃ vastraṃ prādurāsīd anekaśaḥ
> tato halahalāśabdas tatrāsīd ghoranisvanaḥ
> tad adbhutatamaṃ loke vīkṣya sarvamahīkṣitām
> …
> [viduraḥ:] draupadī praśnam uktvaivaṃ roravīti hy anāthavat
> na ca vibrūta taṃ praśnaṃ sabhyā dharmo 'tra pīḍyate
> …
> yo hi praśnaṃ na vibrūyād dharmadarśī sabhāṃ gataḥ
> anṛte yā phalāvāptis tasyāḥ so 'rdhaṃ samaśnute
> yaḥ punar vitathaṃ brūyād dharmadarśī sabhāṃ gataḥ
> anṛtasya phalaṃ kṛtsnaṃ saṃprāpnotīti niścayaḥ

**Literal.** [Karṇa:] Duḥśāsana — this Vikarṇa is a mere boy talking wisdom — take away the garments of the Pāṇḍavas, and of Draupadī too. … Then Duḥśāsana, O king, seizing Draupadī's garment by force in the middle of the assembly, began to drag it off. But as Draupadī's garment was being dragged, O lord of peoples, another garment of the very same form appeared — again and again, many times. Then a roar of tumult arose there, terrible-sounding, as all the earth-lords beheld that greatest marvel in the world. … [Vidura:] Draupadī, having spoken her question thus, wails like one without protector — and you assembly-men do not answer the question: **dharma itself is being wounded here.** … For he who, seeing dharma, enters the assembly and does not answer the question — he eats half the fruit that accrues to untruth (*anṛta*); and he who, seeing dharma, enters the assembly and answers falsely — he obtains the whole fruit of untruth: this is certain.

**Rendering.** Karṇa: "Duḥśāsana — this Vikarṇa is a boy playing at wisdom — strip the Pāṇḍavas of their garments. And hers." The five brothers laid down their upper cloths themselves and sat. Then Duḥśāsana took hold of Draupadī's garment in the middle of the assembly and began to drag. And as the cloth was dragged, cloth of the same form came into being under it — again, and again, and again. A roar went up out of the kings, terrible to hear, as they watched the greatest marvel the world had seen. … And Vidura rose, arms raised over the hall: "She has asked her question and she is howling like a creature with no protector, and you do not answer her. **Dharma itself is being wounded in this hall.** Understand the tariff: the man who sees dharma and sits silent before the question eats *half* the fruit of the lie. The man who sees dharma and answers falsely eats the *whole* of it. That is certain."

**Annotation.** Three things must be said precisely here. **First, about the marvel.** In this critical text no one is named as its agent: the garment simply *prādurāsīt*, "appeared," as an impersonal cosmic event. (The beloved image of Draupadī praying to Kṛṣṇa, who extends her sari from afar, belongs to later expansions of the text and to the devotional tradition; the oldest recoverable reading is starker and, for our purposes, more instructive.) When dharma is wounded past a certain depth, *the cosmos itself refuses the act* — anonymously, mechanically, the way water refuses a flame. No rescuer arrives. Draupadī remains enslaved, humiliated, unanswered; only the one absolute limit holds. **Second, about the men.** The brothers strip *themselves* on Karṇa's word — slaves comply — and it is here that Bhīma, listening to Karṇa, is described by the poem in the decisive phrase you will meet in the next passage: *dharmapāśānubaddhaḥ*, "bound by the nooses of dharma." The paralysis of the hall is not cowardice, and the text closes that exit explicitly: the strongest man alive is shown *tied*, and the rope is named, and the rope is dharma. **Third, about Vidura's tariff.** His half-share rule converts the entire silent assembly into co-debtors of *anṛta* — the un-order, the old Vedic ṛta surviving in its negation. Every king in that hall now carries a fraction of the lie. The audience of the crime has been bookkept into the crime. When, sixteen years later, nearly every man in that room lies dead at Kurukṣetra, the epic expects you to remember this ledger.

*Transition.* Bhīma's bonds deserve their own passage, because in them the epic states its doctrine of dharmic paralysis in a single compound word — and lets the paralyzed man diagnose himself.

### Passage 11: Bound by the Nooses of Dharma
**Mahābhārata 2.63.1, 4, 6–7** — *Karṇa instructs Draupadī in her new status; Bhīma burns.*

> [karṇaḥ:] trayaḥ kileme adhanā bhavanti
> dāsaḥ śiṣyaś cāsvatantrā ca nārī
> dāsasya patnī tvaṃ dhanam asya bhadre
> hīneśvarā dāsadhanaṃ ca dāsī
> …
> parājito nakulo bhīmaseno
> yudhiṣṭhiraḥ sahadevo 'rjunaś ca
> dāsībhūtā praviśa yājñaseni
> parājitās te patayo na santi
> [vaiśaṃpāyanaḥ:] tad vai śrutvā bhīmaseno 'tyamarṣī
> bhṛśaṃ niśaśvāsa tadārtarūpaḥ
> rājānugo dharmapāśānubaddho
> dahann ivainaṃ kopaviraktadṛṣṭiḥ
> [bhīmaḥ:] nāhaṃ kupye sūtaputrasya rājann
> eṣa satyaṃ dāsadharmaḥ praviṣṭaḥ

**Literal.** [Karṇa:] Three, they say, are without property: the slave, the student, and the woman not independent. You are the wife of a slave, his wealth, good lady — mistress-less, a slave-woman, the wealth of a slave. … Defeated are Nakula, Bhīmasena, Yudhiṣṭhira, Sahadeva, and Arjuna. Become a slave, enter [the household], Yājñasenī; your husbands, defeated, *are not*. [Narrator:] Hearing that, Bhīmasena, beyond endurance, breathed hard, his form anguished — **following his king, bound fast by the nooses of dharma** — as if burning him [Karṇa] with a gaze estranged by fury. [Bhīma:] I am not angry at the sūta's son, O king: **it is true — the dharma of slaves has entered into us.**

**Rendering.** Karṇa, to the queen: "Three kinds of person own nothing: the slave, the student, and the dependent woman. Your husband is a slave; you are a slave's property — property of property. Your five husbands are lost, lady. As far as this hall is concerned, *they do not exist*." And Bhīma — the strongest living man, who could have torn the hall down to its foundation stones — heard it, and breathed like a furnace, and did not move: *following his king, bound with the nooses of dharma*, burning Karṇa with his eyes alone. And when he finally spoke, it was to his own brother: "I have no anger for the sūta's son, my king. He is telling the truth. The dharma of slaves has entered into us."

**Annotation.** *Dharmapāśānubaddhaḥ* — "bound-after by the nooses of dharma." The poem could not be more literal: the image is of an animal on a snare-line, and the line is dharma. Trace the bonds one by one, because their overdetermination is the point. Bhīma cannot act because (1) his *svadharma* subordinates him to his eldest brother and king — a warrior who strikes without his king's word is not a hero but a solvent of the entire social order; (2) his king cannot give the word, because his king truthfully said "I am won" and is bound by *dāsadharma* — Karṇa's cruelty is jurisprudentially sound, and Bhīma, with unbearable honesty, *concedes it*: "he speaks the truth"; (3) his king's truthfulness cannot be suspended, because it is the one absolute constituent of the Dharma King's being — recall Bhīṣma: "he would abandon the prosperous earth, but not truth." Every rope is anchored to a virtue. This is what the introduction called crisis-dharma, *āpad-dharma*: not a conflict between good and evil, but a lattice of goods so interlocked that action itself becomes impossible — and *inaction* is meanwhile accruing its half-share of *anṛta* per Vidura's tariff. There is no move that does not wound dharma. The wound, therefore, happens. And a wounded dharma — Passage 3 — strikes back. (Bhīma does one thing with his bound strength: he swears the vows. He will tear open Duḥśāsana's chest and drink his blood; he will shatter Duryodhana's thigh. At Kurukṣetra he does both. In this poem a vow is a deed already begun.)

*Transition.* An omen — jackals howling at midday — finally frightens the blind king into intervening. He grants Draupadī boons; she asks only her husbands' freedom, and pointedly nothing for herself; the Pāṇḍavas are released and sent home with their kingdom. For one page the catastrophe seems averted. Then Duryodhana extracts one more game from his father — a single throw, with exile as the stake — and a messenger overtakes Yudhiṣṭhira on the road.

### Passage 12: The Boat, and the Second Summons
**Mahābhārata 2.64.2–3; 2.67.3–4, 7**

> [karṇaḥ:] krodhāviṣṭeṣu pārtheṣu dhārtarāṣṭreṣu cāpy ati
> draupadī pāṇḍuputrāṇāṃ kṛṣṇā śāntir ihābhavat
> aplave 'mbhasi magnānām apratiṣṭhe nimajjatām
> pāñcālī pāṇḍuputrāṇāṃ naur eṣā pāragābhavat
> …
> [yudhiṣṭhiraḥ:] dhātur niyogād bhūtāni prāpnuvanti śubhāśubham
> na nivṛttis tayor asti devitavyaṃ punar yadi
> akṣadyūte samāhvānaṃ niyogāt sthavirasya ca
> jānann api kṣayakaraṃ nātikramitum utsahe
> [vaiśaṃpāyanaḥ:] yathopajoṣam āsīnāḥ punardyūtapravṛttaye
> sarvalokavināśāya daivenopanipīḍitāḥ

**Literal.** [Karṇa:] When the Pārthas were possessed by wrath, and the Dhārtarāṣṭras exceedingly so, Draupadī — Kṛṣṇā — became the peace of the sons of Pāṇḍu. For men sunk in water without a raft, drowning without footing, the lady of Pāñcāla became the boat of Pāṇḍu's sons, carrying them to the far shore. … [Yudhiṣṭhira:] By the command of the Placer, beings obtain the fair and the foul; there is no turning-away from those two, if it must be diced again. The summons to the dice-game comes by command of the elder: **though knowing it works destruction, I cannot step across it.** [Narrator:] They sat down at their ease for the resumption of the dicing — **for the destruction of the whole world, pressed down by daiva.**

**Rendering.** Even Karṇa said it, in wonder: when both houses stood possessed by wrath, one woman became the peace of the Pāṇḍavas — for men gone under in bottomless water, no raft, no footing, Draupadī was the boat that carried them across. … And on the road home, the second summons came: *the assembly is spread; the dice are cast; your father says, come and play.* And Yudhiṣṭhira answered: "By the Placer's decree beings receive the fair and the foul, and from neither is there any turning away. The summons comes from the elder of my house. **I know it works destruction. I cannot step over it.**" And they turned back, and entered the hall, and sat down in comfort to dice again — *for the destruction of the whole world, ground down by daiva.*

**Annotation.** This is the coda that locks every prior passage into place. Karṇa's boat-image concedes, from the enemy's own mouth, that Draupadī's *guarded dharma* (her word at 2.60.13: "dharma, guarded, will keep peace over us") did precisely what she said it would — she alone, asking for nothing for herself, hauled the whole house of Pāṇḍu back from drowning. Rescue occurred. And then the cosmos reissues the summons — because rescue is not what this story is for. Yudhiṣṭhira's acceptance speech is the epic's dharma-doctrine compressed to two verses: *dhātur niyogāt* (the Placer's decree), *niyogāt sthavirasya* (the elder's command — obedience to the father-figure is itself dharma), and then the sentence a Western ethics cannot metabolize: **jānann api kṣayakaraṃ nātikramitum utsahe** — "though I know it works destruction, I cannot step across it." Knowledge and obligation have come fully apart. Clear sight changes nothing; the vow, the elder, the Placer, and Time all pull one way. And the narrator, closing the movement, zooms out to the register of Passage 4: they sat down to play *sarvalokavināśāya* — "for the destruction of the whole world." The dative of purpose. The earth asked to be unburdened; the players have taken their seats. The single throw is lost; the Pāṇḍavas go to the forest for thirteen years; Draupadī goes with her hair unbound, vowing to bind it only when it has been washed in Duḥśāsana's blood.

## CONCLUSION: HOW THE STORY WILL NOW READ

If this edition has done its work, the narrative that follows the dicing hall — the thirteen years of exile, the failed embassies, the eighteen days of Kurukṣetra — will read to you not as a revenge saga but as **structural mechanics**, and you will find that this makes it more terrible, not less.

**The war is not vengeance; it is recoil.** *Dharmo hi hato hanti* — dharma, struck down, strikes down (Passage 3). Dharma was struck in the hall: a queen's question unanswered (Passage 9), her body assaulted before elders who sat silent, each silent man eating his half-share of *anṛta* (Passage 10). From that moment the recoil is in motion, and everything afterward — Kṛṣṇa's failed peace embassy, the omens, the eighteen armies — is the striking-back traveling through the structure. The traditional accounting of the dead at Kurukṣetra is given by the epic in numbers meant to stagger (the Strī Parva counts over a billion and a half slain); the sober measure is eighteen *akṣauhiṇīs* — on the epic's own arithmetic, some four million warriors — and nearly every named man who sat in that hall is among them. That is not a judge's sentence. It is a roof coming down on everyone who stood under it, which is what the assembly, by its silence, chose to hold up.

**The paralysis was the crime, and also the dharma.** The alien thing you must carry into the war narrative is that both halves of that sentence are true. Bhīma bound by the nooses of dharma (Passage 11) was *right* to be bound — every rope was a virtue — and the hall's inaction still wounded the cosmos and still incurred the debt. Western moral grammar demands that one of these be false: either they couldn't act (and are innocent) or they could (and are guilty). The Mahābhārata's grammar allows *bound and liable*. When, in the Bhagavad Gītā — spoken on the war's first morning, and lying beyond this edition's two books — Arjuna drops his bow and refuses to kill his teachers and kin, he is having the dicing-hall crisis over again in reverse: this time *action* is the horror and dharma commands it, and Kṛṣṇa's answer ("your entitlement is to the act alone, never to its fruits") is the same doctrine Yudhiṣṭhira spoke on the road: the deed is yours; the destruction it works belongs to the Placer.

**The demolition was scheduled.** Keep Passage 4 under everything. The earth, too heavy with adharma, asked to be unburdened, and the gods were born by portions *virodhāya* — "for the collision." The dice game is how the collision was triggered; the war is how the weight came off. When you read Kṛṣṇa — who sat in that hall's world and did not prevent the game, who steers the war and bends its rules — do not read a god who failed to intervene. Read the descent itself: the unburdening, walking on two feet. And when you reach the epic's end, where the victorious Yudhiṣṭhira feels no triumph, only ash, and rules a kingdom of widows before walking into the Himalaya — you will recognize the final proof of everything taught here. Dharma was restored. The earth was lightened. The wheel of Passage 1 turned into the Kali Yuga, as it always does, as it always will. No one was saved, because saving is not what dharma does. **Dharma upholds.** What it upholds is not you.

The reader who has absorbed these twelve passages will hear the dice fall as they should be heard: not as a plot device, but as the sound of a perfectly ordered universe doing, without malice and without mercy, exactly what it is built to do.

## ASSESSMENT (Editorial — for the publication team, not for the reader-facing text)

**Verification of the philosophy against the Sanskrit.** The exposition's core claims survive cross-examination and are now *better* supported than it claimed: the Kshatriya cannot-refuse doctrine, which the exposition supported with an unattributed paraphrase quote, is replaced by actual verses (2.44.18, 2.52.16, 2.67.3–4); "dharmic paralysis" is grounded in the text's own words (*dharmapāśānubaddhaḥ* 2.63.6, *dharmo 'tra pīḍyate* 2.61.52, *dharmasaukṣmyāt* 2.60.40); the "mechanical, non-punitive" thesis is grounded in *dharmo hi hato hanti* (1.37.22), the daiva verses (2.52.18, 2.67.7), and the earth-burden pun on √dhṛ (1.58.29).

**Corrections made to the specialist inputs (five over-interpretations/errors):**
1. **Krishna and the sari.** The exposition states Krishna protects Draupadī's garment. In the BORI critical edition (2.61.41) the garments appear with *no agent named*; the Kṛṣṇa intervention is vulgate/devotional. Corrected, and the starker critical reading is put to pedagogical use.
2. **"Shakuni demands Draupadi be brought."** It is Śakuni who proposes the *stake* (2.58.31) but *Duryodhana* who orders her brought (2.60.10, 2.60.18). Corrected.
3. **"2 million killed."** The epic's own figure (Strī Parva) is ~1.66 billion; the standard sober measure is 18 akṣauhiṇīs ≈ 4 million. The conclusion now states both honestly.
4. **"Assembled kings bound by guest-dharma."** Not attested in the extracted text; the actual textual mechanisms of paralysis (question-subtlety, king-obedience, dāsadharma, daiva, Vidura's silence-tariff) are used instead.
5. **"In Rumi's own words"** (exposition, final line) is a copy-paste error from another project; the exposition's mechanical/moral contrast is also slightly over-flattened — the text dramatizes dharma as *contested and subtle* (Draupadī's unanswered question), not as a settled doctrine; the final work preserves the debate.
