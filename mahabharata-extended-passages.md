# THE MAHĀBHĀRATA'S UNFOLDING
## Extended Passages in Translation

*Sanskrit: Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute Critical Edition (Pune, 1927–1966), Ādiparvan and Sabhāparvan. Transliteration: IAST. Translations original to this edition.*

---

### A note on what this is

Six long stretches of the epic, translated at length, in the order the poem gives them. The Sanskrit is quoted in samples — enough to show the texture and the rhythm of the ślokas, not every word — and the English follows the syntax rather than smoothing it into paraphrase.

The Mahābhārata does not deliver its ideas as statements. It delivers them as *accumulation*. The earth is not simply declared overburdened; the poem spends fifty verses describing a world so well-ordered that nothing dies young, and then shows that order becoming the mechanism of the overload. Yudhiṣṭhira does not simply lose; he loses one named thing after another, and the naming is the point.

Two words are left untranslated. **Dharma** is what holds a thing in its place and in its function — cosmic, social, personal at once — and its root, √dhṛ, "to bear, to hold up," will do real work in the first passage. **Daiva** is what has been apportioned: fate, the divinely allotted. The characters invoke it constantly, and the reader should watch *when* they invoke it.

## I. THE WEIGHT OF THE WORLD

**Ādi Parva 1.58.1–51** — Janamejaya, at whose snake-sacrifice the epic is being recited, asks his teacher why the heroes he has been hearing about were born at all.

Janamejaya asks it plainly (1.58.2):

> yadartham iha saṃbhūtā devakalpā mahārathāḥ
> bhuvi tan me mahābhāga samyag ākhyātum arhasi

*"For what purpose were these godlike great chariot-warriors brought into being on the earth? Tell me that, fortunate one, and tell it rightly."*

And Vaiśaṃpāyana answers that the thing being asked about is a secret belonging to the gods — *rahasyaṃ khalv idaṃ rājan devānām iti naḥ śrutam* — which he will tell only after bowing to the Self-Existent.

He begins very far back. Rāma son of Jamadagni had emptied the earth of its warrior class twenty-one times over and then gone to Mount Mahendra to do austerities. With the kṣatriyas gone, kṣatriya women went to brahmins wanting children, and the brahmins came to them (1.58.6):

> tābhiḥ saha samāpetur brāhmaṇāḥ saṃśitavratāḥ
> ṛtāv ṛtau naravyāghra na kāmān nānṛtau tathā

*"Brahmins of severe vows came together with them season by fertile season, tiger among men — not out of desire, and not out of season."*

Thousands of warrior-caste women conceived by them and bore sons and daughters, and the warrior class was restored — but restored, the text insists, *dharmeṇa*, by dharma, and it therefore came back stronger than it had been. This is the crucial hinge, and the poem lingers on it for twenty verses. Because the new kṣatriya class was begotten in order and not in appetite, everything downstream of it went right. Kings punished only the punishable, and by dharma. Indra of the thousand eyes rained sweetly, in the right place at the right time, and the people swelled. Brahmins studied the Vedas with their limbs and their Upaniṣads, and did not sell their learning. Farmers did not yoke thin oxen, and did not milk calves still on the froth. Merchants did not weigh with false weights (1.58.20–22):

> na kūṭamānair vaṇijaḥ paṇyaṃ vikrīṇate tadā
> karmāṇi ca naravyāghra dharmopetāni mānavāḥ
> dharmam evānupaśyantaś cakrur dharmaparāyaṇāḥ
> svakarmaniratāś cāsan sarve varṇā narādhipa
> evaṃ tadā naravyāghra dharmo na hrasate kva cit

*"Then the traders did not sell their goods by false measures. Human beings did their works joined to dharma; looking upon dharma alone, devoted to dharma, they acted. Every class delighted in its own proper work, O king — and so, tiger among men, dharma did not diminish anywhere."*

Cows calved on time and women bore on time; trees flowered and fruited in their seasons; nobody died a child, and nobody knew a woman before he had reached his youth. Then comes the sentence the whole passage has been building toward (1.58.24):

> evaṃ kṛtayuge samyag vartamāne tadā nṛpa
> āpūryata mahī kṛtsnā prāṇibhir bahubhir bhṛśam

*"Thus, while the Kṛta age was going correctly, the whole earth was filled — filled to bursting — with living beings."*

The perfection is the problem. Nothing has gone wrong. Dharma has been observed exactly, and the exact observance of dharma has produced a world with no room left in it.

Into that saturated world the demons come — not by invasion, but by birth. Defeated repeatedly by the Ādityas and thrown down from their sovereignty, the asuras began to be born *in the wombs of kings* (1.58.25–28):

> tataḥ samudite loke mānuṣe bharatarṣabha
> asurā jajñire kṣetre rājñāṃ manujapuṃgava
> ādityair hi tadā daityā bahuśo nirjitā yudhi
> aiśvaryād bhraṃśitāś cāpi saṃbabhūvuḥ kṣitāv iha
> …
> goṣv aśveṣu ca rājendra kharoṣṭramahiṣeṣu ca
> kravyādeṣu ca bhūteṣu gajeṣu ca mṛgeṣu ca

*"Then, when the human world had risen to its height, bull of the Bharatas, the asuras were born in the field of kings. Beaten again and again in battle by the Ādityas, thrown down from their lordship, they came to be here on the earth — among cattle and horses, among asses and camels and buffalo, among the flesh-eating creatures, among elephants and among deer."*

They came wanting divinity, and took whatever body was available. And now the line that names the disease (1.58.29):

> jātair iha mahīpāla jāyamānaiś ca tair mahī
> na śaśākātmanātmānam iyaṃ dhārayituṃ dharā

*"With those already born here and those still being born, O protector of the earth — this earth, the Bearer, was no longer able by herself to bear herself up."*

The pun is not decoration. *Dharā*, "she who bears," cannot perform *dhārayitum*, "to bear" — and both come from the root that gives *dharma*, "that which holds up." The epic's diagnosis of what has gone wrong with the world is not that it has become wicked. It is that it has become **too heavy to hold**.

What the newborn demons then do is described as ordinary tyranny at enormous scale. Proud, strong, wearing whatever forms they chose, they went over the sea-bounded earth crushing brahmin, kṣatriya, vaiśya and śūdra alike, and every other kind of being with them; they terrified and slaughtered and roamed by the hundred thousand; they violated the great seers in their hermitages, being irreverent, drunk on strength and drunk on the drunkenness of strength: *abrahmaṇyā vīryamadā mattā madabalena ca* (1.58.34).

Then the passage lifts into its central image (1.58.36–37):

> na hīmāṃ pavano rājan na nāgā na nagā mahīm
> tadā dhārayituṃ śekur ākrāntāṃ dānavair balāt
> tato mahī mahīpāla bhārārtā bhayapīḍitā
> jagāma śaraṇaṃ devaṃ sarvabhūtapitāmaham

*"The wind could not hold this earth up, king; the world-serpents could not; the mountains could not — overrun by the Dānavas, and overrun by force. Then the earth, pained by her burden and crushed by fear, went for refuge to the god who is grandfather of all beings."*

She found Brahmā surrounded by gods and twice-born and great seers, praised by gandharvas and apsarases who are professional in the work of praising, and she bowed and made her petition. And the poem stops to say something important about that petition (1.58.41–42):

> tat pradhānātmanas tasya bhūmeḥ kṛtyaṃ svayaṃbhuvaḥ
> pūrvam evābhavad rājan viditaṃ parameṣṭhinaḥ
> sraṣṭā hi jagataḥ kasmān na saṃbudhyeta bhārata
> surāsurāṇāṃ lokānām aśeṣeṇa manogatam

*"The earth's business was already known beforehand to that Self-Existent one, whose nature is the first principle, the Highest-Standing. For why would the maker of the world not know, Bhārata — completely, and without remainder — what is in the minds of the gods, the asuras, and the worlds?"*

He knew before she came. She came anyway. The epic will keep doing this: showing us that the outcome is fixed and that the approach to it still has to be walked.

Brahmā's answer is short (1.58.44):

> yadartham asi saṃprāptā matsakāśaṃ vasuṃdhare
> tadarthaṃ saṃniyokṣyāmi sarvān eva divaukasaḥ

*"For the purpose for which you have come into my presence, treasure-bearing one — for that purpose I shall commission every last one of the dwellers in heaven."*

He dismissed the earth and gave the order (1.58.46–47):

> asyā bhūmer nirasituṃ bhāraṃ bhāgaiḥ pṛthak pṛthak
> asyām eva prasūyadhvaṃ virodhāyeti cābravīt
> …
> svair aṃśaiḥ saṃprasūyadhvaṃ yatheṣṭaṃ mānuṣeṣv iti

*"To cast off the burden of this earth, each by his own separate portion — be born upon her, for conflict. So he said. … And to the gandharvas and apsarases assembled he spoke this excellent word: 'By your own portions be born among human beings, as you please.'"*

**Virodhāya.** For conflict. For collision. That is the stated purpose, given as an instruction, in advance.

Śakra and the rest heard the word of the guru of the gods and took it up as true, as advantageous, and as wholesome — *tathyam arthyaṃ ca pathyaṃ ca* — and then, ready to go down each by his own portion, they went to Nārāyaṇa at Vaikuṇṭha, the dark-lustred one in yellow cloth with wheel and mace in hand. And Indra said to him (1.58.51):

> taṃ bhuvaḥ śodhanāyendra uvāca puruṣottamam
> aṃśenāvatarasveti tathety āha ca taṃ hariḥ

*"For the cleansing of the earth Indra said to the highest of persons, 'Descend by a portion' — and Hari said to him, 'So be it.'"*

*The war has now been ordered. What remains is to distribute it into bodies.*

## II. THE DESCENT BY PORTIONS

**Ādi Parva 1.61.63–102; 1.113.23–43; 1.114.1–38; 1.115.1–28** — the roster of who is whose portion, and then the scene of the actual births.

Janamejaya wants the list. Vaiśaṃpāyana gives it, and it runs for a hundred verses: this demon became that king, this Kālakeya became that lord of the Niṣādas. It reads like a muster roll, because it is one. Then it arrives at the people we care about, and the entries begin to sting.

Droṇa, the teacher of both sides, is a portion of Bṛhaspati — begotten from no womb (*ayonija*), the best of all who know weapons. His son Aśvatthāman comes of a stranger source (1.61.66–67):

> mahādevāntakābhyāṃ ca kāmāt krodhāc ca bhārata
> ekatvam upapannānāṃ jajñe śūraḥ paraṃtapaḥ
> aśvatthāmā mahāvīryaḥ śatrupakṣakṣayaṃkaraḥ

*"From Mahādeva and from Death, from Desire and from Anger — from those four gone into a single unity — was born the hero, the scorcher of foes: Aśvatthāman of great strength, the annihilator of the enemy's side."*

The eight Vasus were born as Gaṅgā's sons by Śaṃtanu, by Vasiṣṭha's curse and Indra's command, and the last-born of them is Bhīṣma. Kṛpa is out of the host of the Rudras. And then, with no comment at all (1.61.72):

> śakunir nāma yas tv āsīd rājā loke mahārathaḥ
> dvāparaṃ viddhi taṃ rājan saṃbhūtam arimardanam

*"The king called Śakuni, the great chariot-warrior in the world — know him, O king, to have come into being as Dvāpara: the crusher of enemies."*

Dvāpara is not a person. It is *the third age of the world*, and it is also the losing throw of the dice. The man who will run the dice game is the age itself, wearing a body. His nephew is worse (1.61.80–81):

> kaler aṃśāt tu saṃjajñe bhuvi duryodhano nṛpaḥ
> durbuddhir durmatiś caiva kurūṇām ayaśaskaraḥ
> jagato yaḥ sa sarvasya vidviṣṭaḥ kalipūruṣaḥ
> yaḥ sarvāṃ ghātayām āsa pṛthivīṃ puruṣādhamaḥ
> yena vairaṃ samuddīptaṃ bhūtāntakaraṇaṃ mahat

*"From a portion of Kali was born on earth the king Duryodhana — ill-minded, ill-judging, the unmaker of the Kurus' fame; hated by all the world, the man of the Kali age, the lowest of men, who had the whole earth slaughtered, by whom was kindled that great enmity that makes an end of beings."*

His hundred brothers, Duḥśāsana at their head, are rākṣasas of Pulastya's line. Dhṛtarāṣṭra is a gandharva king born blind of his mother's fault and a seer's anger. Vidura is the son of Atri, wisest of the wise.

And then the five (1.61.84–85):

> dharmasyāṃśaṃ tu rājānaṃ viddhi rājan yudhiṣṭhiram
> bhīmasenaṃ tu vātasya devarājasya cārjunam
> aśvinos tu tathaivāṃśau rūpeṇāpratimau bhuvi
> nakulaḥ sahadevaś ca sarvalokamanoharau

*"Know the king Yudhiṣṭhira to be the portion of Dharma; Bhīmasena of the Wind; Arjuna of the king of the gods. And the two portions of the Aśvins, unmatched on earth in their beauty — Nakula and Sahadeva, the delight of all the world."*

Karṇa is the portion of the Sun. Kṛṣṇa is the portion of Nārayaṇa himself, the eternal god of gods. Dhṛṣṭadyumna is a portion of Fire. Śikhaṇḍin, the text says flatly, is a rākṣasa, and *strīpuṃsa* — woman-and-man.

Draupadī's entry is the strangest, and it should be read against everything that will happen to her (1.61.95–97):

> śriyas tu bhāgaḥ saṃjajñe ratyarthaṃ pṛthivītale
> drupadasya kule kanyā vedimadhyād aninditā
> nātihrasvā na mahatī nīlotpalasugandhinī
> padmāyatākṣī suśroṇī asitāyatamūrdhajā
> sarvalakṣaṇasaṃpannā vaiḍūryamaṇisaṃnibhā
> pañcānāṃ puruṣendrāṇāṃ cittapramathinī rahaḥ

*"A portion of Śrī — of Fortune herself — was born on the surface of the earth for the sake of delight: a blameless girl in Drupada's line, out of the middle of the sacrificial altar. Not too short, not too tall, fragrant as the blue lotus, long-eyed as the lotus, with long dark hair; endowed with every auspicious mark, like a beryl gem; the churner of the hearts of five lords of men."*

Fortune, born out of a fire-altar. When she is dragged into the hall, what is being seized is the presence of prosperity in the Kuru house — which is exactly the thing Duryodhana went to Indraprastha and could not stop looking at. And the mothers close the list: Siddhi and Dhṛti, Success and Steadfastness, were born as Kuntī and Mādrī; Mati, Judgment, was born as Subala's daughter, Gāndhārī.

Vaiśaṃpāyana closes: *"Thus has the descent by portions been recited"* — *aṃśāvataraṇaṃ rājan* — and adds that a wise man who hears it, and who knows origin and dissolution, *na kṛcchreṣv avasīdati*: "does not sink down in hard times."

*The roster exists. Now the poem has to get these portions into bodies — and here it slows down, because getting them into bodies requires a childless king to argue his wife into something.*

By the time we reach 1.113, Pāṇḍu has been cursed to die if he ever approaches a woman in desire. He has no sons. He is arguing with Kuntī, and his argument is a legal-historical one: he tells her that women were once unconfined, that this was itself the ancient dharma, that the restriction we now live under was imposed relatively recently and in anger by a particular man, Śvetaketu son of Uddālaka, who could not bear watching a brahmin lead his mother away by the hand. Pāṇḍu ends (1.113.28–30):

> viśeṣataḥ putragṛddhī hīnaḥ prajananāt svayam
> yathāham anavadyāṅgi putradarśanalālasaḥ
> …
> manniyogāt sukeśānte dvijātes tapasādhikāt
> putrān guṇasamāyuktān utpādayitum arhasi
> tvatkṛte 'haṃ pṛthuśroṇi gaccheyaṃ putriṇāṃ gatim

*"— especially a man greedy for a son, cut off himself from begetting, as I am; longing to look at a son. … At my commission, then, by a twice-born man superior in austerity, you should bring forth sons endowed with every quality. Through you I would go to the destination of those who have sons."*

He is asking with his hands joined at his head, and he says so.

And Kuntī answers with a secret she has been carrying since she was a girl serving guests in her father's house (1.113.33–36):

> nigūḍhaniścayaṃ dharme yaṃ taṃ durvāsasaṃ viduḥ
> tam ahaṃ saṃśitātmānaṃ sarvayatnair atoṣayam
> sa me 'bhicārasaṃyuktam ācaṣṭa bhagavān varam
> mantragrāmaṃ ca me prādād abravīc caiva mām idam
> yaṃ yaṃ devaṃ tvam etena mantreṇāvāhayiṣyasi
> akāmo vā sakāmo vā sa te vaśam upaiṣyati

*"The one they know as Durvāsas, whose resolve in dharma is hidden — that man of honed self I satisfied with every effort. And the blessed one declared to me a boon joined with power of compulsion, and gave me a body of mantras, and said this to me: 'Whichever god you summon with this mantra, willing or unwilling, that god will come into your power.'"*

The mechanism is an instrument of coercion. She then asks his permission and asks him to choose which god. He answers without hesitating (1.113.39–42):

> adyaiva tvaṃ varārohe prayatasva yathāvidhi
> dharmam āvāhaya śubhe sa hi deveṣu puṇyabhāk
> adharmeṇa na no dharmaḥ saṃyujyeta kathaṃ cana
> lokaś cāyaṃ varārohe dharmo 'yam iti maṃsyate

*"This very day, prepare yourself according to the rule. Summon Dharma, auspicious one — for he is the one among the gods who holds the share of merit. In no way let our dharma be joined to adharma; and this world, too, will judge the thing to be dharma."*

He chooses Dharma first partly out of piety and partly out of public relations, and he says both halves out loud.

**The births** (1.114.1–13). In the year when Gāndhārī's own pregnancy is already a year old, Kuntī makes the offering and recites the mantra given by Durvāsas, and joins with Dharma in his yoga-body.

> aindre candrasamāyukte muhūrte 'bhijite 'ṣṭame
> divā madhyagate sūrye tithau puṇye 'bhipūjite
> samṛddhayaśasaṃ kuntī suṣāva samaye sutam
> jātamātre sute tasmin vāg uvācāśarīriṇī
> eṣa dharmabhṛtāṃ śreṣṭho bhaviṣyati na saṃśayaḥ
> yudhiṣṭhira iti khyātaḥ pāṇḍoḥ prathamajaḥ sutaḥ

*"In Indra's asterism, joined with the moon, in the eighth hour, the Abhijit hour, with the sun standing at midday, on a pure and honored lunar day, Kuntī bore in due time a son of abundant fame. And the moment the son was born, a bodiless voice spoke: 'This one will be the best of the upholders of dharma, without doubt — known as Yudhiṣṭhira, the first-born son of Pāṇḍu.'"*

Pāṇḍu's response to a son who will be best of the upholders of dharma is immediate and characteristic: *prāhuḥ kṣatraṃ balajyeṣṭhaṃ balajyeṣṭhaṃ sutaṃ vṛṇu* — *"They say the warrior class has strength for its eldest. Choose a son whose eldest quality is strength."* So she called the Wind, and Bhīma was born, and the voice said he would be the first of all the strong. Then the detail the poem cannot resist: Kuntī, startled by a tiger, leapt up without realizing the infant was asleep in her lap, and the child fell on a rock —

> tataḥ sa vajrasaṃghātaḥ kumāro 'bhyapatad girau
> patatā tena śatadhā śilā gātrair vicūrṇitā

*"— then that boy, whose body was a mass of thunderbolt, fell on the mountain; and by his falling the rock was ground to powder, into a hundred pieces, by his limbs."*

On the same day Bhīma was born, Duryodhana was born too.

Then Pāṇḍu wants more, and here the poem states its own physics (1.114.15–18):

> jāte vṛkodare pāṇḍur idaṃ bhūyo 'nvacintayat
> kathaṃ nu me varaḥ putro lokaśreṣṭho bhaved iti
> daive puruṣakāre ca loko 'yaṃ hi pratiṣṭhitaḥ
> tatra daivaṃ tu vidhinā kālayuktena labhyate

*"When Wolf-belly had been born, Pāṇḍu thought further: 'How might I have a still better son, the best in the world?' — For this world stands upon two things: on daiva, and on the effort of a man. Of these, daiva is obtained by a rite conjoined with the right time."*

So he did the rite. He and Kuntī took a year's vow; he stood on one foot in fierce austerity, turning with the sun. At length Indra spoke to him and promised a son famed in the three worlds, and Kuntī summoned Indra, and Arjuna was born. And now the bodiless voice becomes a chorus. The Ādityas stand in the sky increasing the child's greatness; the eleven Rudras take their station; the Aśvins, the eight Vasus, the Maruts, the Viśvedevas and the Sādhyas stand about; Karkoṭaka and Śeṣa and Vāsuki and Takṣaka come; Tārkṣya and Garuḍa come; sixteen named gandharvas sing and thirty-odd named apsarases dance, and the seven great seers arrive by name. The prophecy runs eight verses and includes, among other things, the burning of the Khāṇḍava forest, the acquisition of every divine weapon, and the recovery of a vanished fortune: *vipranaṣṭāṃ śriyaṃ cāyam āhartā puruṣarṣabhaḥ* — *"and this bull among men will be the one who brings back the fortune that was utterly lost."*

Read that promise now, and read it again after the dice.

The last two are Mādrī's, and they come out of ordinary domestic grief. She tells Pāṇḍu she can bear his infirmity and can bear being the junior wife; what she cannot bear is being childless while her co-wife has children. Pāṇḍu argues Kuntī into sharing the mantra — *"carry Mādrī across as if on a raft"* — and Kuntī tells her to think of a god, once. Mādrī thinks of the Aśvins, both of them, and bears twins, Nakula and Sahadeva, matchless on earth in their beauty. When Pāṇḍu presses Kuntī to let her do it again, Kuntī refuses in a single dry sentence (1.115.23–24):

> uktā sakṛd dvandvam eṣā lebhe tenāsmi vañcitā
> bibhemy asyāḥ paribhavān nārīṇāṃ gatir īdṛśī
> nājñāsiṣam ahaṃ mūḍhā dvandvāhvāne phaladvayam

*"Told once, she got a pair — and by that I have been cheated. I am afraid of being outdone by her; such is the way women go. Fool that I was, I did not understand that summoning the pair would yield a double fruit."*

The gods' war-plan has been executed, and it has been executed through a curse, a coercive mantra, a scriptural argument about ancient sexual custom, a competitive co-wife, and a woman's fear of losing rank in her own household. That is how *aṃśāvataraṇa* actually happens.

> evaṃ pāṇḍoḥ sutāḥ pañca devadattā mahābalāḥ
> saṃbhūtāḥ kīrtimantas te kuruvaṃśavivardhanāḥ

*"So Pāṇḍu's five sons, god-given and of great strength, came into being: renowned, the increasers of the Kuru line."* They grew on the holy Himālayan mountain, and the great seers who came there marvelled at them. The five, and the hundred, grew up quickly, *apsv iva nīrajāḥ* — "like lotuses in water."

## III. WHAT DURYODHANA SAW

**Sabhā Parva 2.43.1–2.45.58; 2.50.10–28; 2.51.1–26** — after the Rājasūya, in the assembly hall Maya built.

*Between the last passage and this one lie the childhood rivalries, the house of lac, the exile, the winning of Draupadī, the partition of the kingdom, the burning of the Khāṇḍava forest, and Yudhiṣṭhira's imperial consecration. The gods' collision has not yet been arranged. It is arranged here, and it begins with a man slipping on a floor.*

Duryodhana had been staying in the hall, walking through it slowly with Śakuni, seeing marvels he had never seen in Hāstinapura. And then (2.43.3–6):

> sa kadā cit sabhāmadhye dhārtarāṣṭro mahīpatiḥ
> sphāṭikaṃ talam āsādya jalam ity abhiśaṅkayā
> svavastrotkarṣaṇaṃ rājā kṛtavān buddhimohitaḥ
> durmanā vimukhaś caiva paricakrāma tāṃ sabhām
> tataḥ sphāṭikatoyāṃ vai sphāṭikāmbujaśobhitām
> vāpīṃ matvā sthalam iti savāsāḥ prāpataj jale

*"Once, in the middle of the hall, that lord of earth came upon a crystal floor and, suspecting it was water, hitched up his clothes — his mind deceived. Then, sick at heart and turning his face away, he went on through the hall. And then, taking a pool of crystal-clear water bright with crystal lotuses to be solid ground, he fell into it with his clothes on."*

The servants laughed at him and, on the king's order, brought him fresh clothes. Bhīma laughed, and Arjuna, and both the twins. He could not bear it, and did not look at them; he kept his face composed. Then he lifted his clothes again to cross what was in fact dry floor, and they laughed again. Then he walked into a doorway that was open, thinking it shut, and struck it with his forehead.

Having been made a fool of in these various ways, he was given leave by the Pāṇḍavas and went back to the City of the Elephant. And on the road (2.43.13):

> pāṇḍavaśrīprataptasya dhyānaglānasya gacchataḥ
> duryodhanasya nṛpateḥ pāpā matir ajāyata

*"As he went — scorched by the Pāṇḍavas' fortune, worn out by brooding — an evil thought was born in king Duryodhana."*

Śakuni notices him sighing and asks what the root of it is, and what follows is one of the great speeches of the epic. It is worth having entire, because it is the psychological ground of everything after (2.43.19–34):

> dṛṣṭvemāṃ pṛthivīṃ kṛtsnāṃ yudhiṣṭhiravaśānugām
> jitām astrapratāpena śvetāśvasya mahātmanaḥ
> …
> amarṣeṇa susaṃpūrṇo dahyamāno divāniśam
> śuciśukrāgame kāle śuṣye toyam ivālpakam

*"Having seen this whole earth gone under Yudhiṣṭhira's control, conquered by the blaze of the great-souled White-Horsed one's weapons; having seen that sacrifice of Pṛthā's son, uncle — as Indra's is among the gods, such was it — I am filled to the brim with a rage I cannot swallow, burning day and night. In the season when the hot months come I dry up like a little water."*

He runs through what he saw. Śiśupāla cut down by Kṛṣṇa in open court, and not one man there who would take a step for him. Kings who were themselves being burnt by the fire that rises off the Pāṇḍavas, and who endured the outrage — "and who is fit to endure it?" Kings bringing jewels of every kind and standing before Kuntī's son *vaiśyā iva karapradāḥ*, "like tradesmen paying tax."

> śriyaṃ tathāvidhāṃ dṛṣṭvā jvalantīm iva pāṇḍave
> amarṣavaśam āpanno dahye 'ham atathocitaḥ
> vahnim eva pravekṣyāmi bhakṣayiṣyāmi vā viṣam
> apo vāpi pravekṣyāmi na hi śakṣyāmi jīvitum

*"Having seen a fortune of that kind, all but blazing, on the son of Pāṇḍu, I have fallen into the power of a rage I cannot bear, and I burn, unaccustomed to this. I will enter fire, or I will eat poison, or I will walk into water — I am not going to be able to live."*

Then the sentence that is the hinge of his character (2.43.29):

> so 'haṃ na strī na cāpy astrī na pumān nāpumān api
> yo 'haṃ tāṃ marṣayāmy adya tādṛśīṃ śriyam āgatām

*"So I am not a woman, and not a non-woman; not a man, and not a non-man either — I, who today put up with a fortune of that sort having come to another."*

He is not describing envy. He is describing the collapse of a category. Then, twice, in almost the same words (2.43.31–34):

> aśaktaś caika evāhaṃ tām āhartuṃ nṛpaśriyam
> sahāyāṃś ca na paśyāmi tena mṛtyuṃ vicintaye
> daivam eva paraṃ manye pauruṣaṃ tu nirarthakam
> …
> kṛto yatno mayā pūrvaṃ vināśe tasya saubala
> tac ca sarvam atikramya sa vṛddho 'psv iva paṅkajam
> tena daivaṃ paraṃ manye pauruṣaṃ tu nirarthakam
> dhārtarāṣṭrā hi hīyante pārthā vardhanti nityaśaḥ

*"Alone, I am not able to take that royal fortune, and I see no allies; therefore I am thinking about death. I hold daiva to be supreme, and a man's own effort to be pointless. … Effort was made by me before, uncle, toward his destruction — and he passed clean through all of it and grew, like a lotus in water. So I hold daiva to be supreme, and a man's own effort to be pointless: the sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra dwindle, and Pṛthā's sons increase perpetually."*

Duryodhana has just tried to murder his cousins and failed, and draws from that failure the conclusion that *fate rules and effort is worthless* — word for word the doctrine his blind father will use to authorize the dice game. In this epic, "daiva is supreme" is almost never said by someone at peace. It is what men say when they are about to do something and want it not to be theirs.

Śakuni's reply is cool. Don't rage at Yudhiṣṭhira; the Pāṇḍavas are simply eating their own allotted portions, *bhāgadheyāni hi svāni pāṇḍavā bhuñjate sadā*. You tried repeatedly and they got free every time because their portion protected them. They won Draupadī and Drupada and his sons, and mighty Vāsudeva as an ally; Arjuna got Gāṇḍīva and two inexhaustible quivers and divine weapons by satisfying the Fire; with that bow and the strength of his arms he made the kings of earth submit. What is there to weep about? And as for having no allies — that's false. You have Droṇa and his clever son; you have Rādhā's son the charioteer's boy; you have Kṛpa; you have me and my brothers and Somadatta's son (2.44.11):

> etais tvaṃ sahitaḥ sarvair jaya kṛtsnāṃ vasuṃdharām

*"Joined with all of these, conquer the whole earth."*

Duryodhana takes it literally — *let us go and beat them* — and Śakuni corrects him with the sentence that produces the rest of the Mahābhārata (2.44.14–19):

> dhanaṃjayo vāsudevo bhīmaseno yudhiṣṭhiraḥ
> nakulaḥ sahadevaś ca drupadaś ca sahātmajaiḥ
> naite yudhi balāj jetuṃ śakyāḥ suragaṇair api
> …
> dyūtapriyaś ca kaunteyo na ca jānāti devitum
> samāhūtaś ca rājendro na śakṣyati nivartitum
> devane kuśalaś cāhaṃ na me 'sti sadṛśo bhuvi
> triṣu lokeṣu kaunteyaṃ taṃ tvaṃ dyūte samāhvaya

*"Arjuna, Vāsudeva, Bhīmasena, Yudhiṣṭhira, Nakula, Sahadeva, and Drupada with his sons — these are not to be beaten in battle by force, not even by the hosts of the gods. … But the son of Kuntī loves dicing and does not know how to play; and once he is challenged, that lord of kings will not be able to refuse. And I am skilled at the game, and I have no equal on earth. Summon that son of Kuntī to dice — in the three worlds, you summon him."*

The plan is not to cheat a man out of his kingdom. The plan is to locate the single place where a man's virtue is also a lock that opens from the outside. Yudhiṣṭhira's vow — *"summoned, I do not turn back"* — is not a weakness in his dharma. It is his dharma. Śakuni has found the door by reading the dharma correctly.

Then it has to be sold to the old king. Śakuni goes first and tells Dhṛtarāṣṭra that his eldest son has gone pale and thin and wretched, and asks why he does not look into it. Dhṛtarāṣṭra's bewilderment is genuine and slightly awful (2.45.8–11):

> aiśvaryaṃ hi mahat putra tvayi sarvaṃ samarpitam
> bhrātaraḥ suhṛdaś caiva nācaranti tavāpriyam
> ācchādayasi prāvārān aśnāsi piśitaudanam
> ājāneyā vahanti tvāṃ kenāsi hariṇaḥ kṛśaḥ

*"Great lordship, my son, has all been handed over to you. Your brothers and friends do nothing you dislike. You put on fine cloaks; you eat rice with meat; thoroughbreds carry you. Why are you sallow and thin?"*

And Duryodhana answers with a whole theory of kingship (2.45.12–16):

> aśnāmy ācchādaye cāhaṃ yathā kupuruṣas tathā
> amarṣaṃ dhāraye cograṃ titikṣan kālaparyayam
> amarṣaṇaḥ svāḥ prakṛtīr abhibhūya pare sthitāḥ
> kleśān mumukṣuḥ parajān sa vai puruṣa ucyate
> saṃtoṣo vai śriyaṃ hanti abhimānaś ca bhārata

*"I eat and I dress the way a worthless man does. I carry a fierce unbearable anger, waiting out the turning of time. The man who cannot bear it, who overcomes his own natural constraints while the enemy stands over him, who wants to be free of the sufferings the enemy causes — he is what is called a man. Contentment kills fortune, Bhārata, and so does self-satisfaction."*

Then comes the inventory, which is the real argument, and which the poem gives at length precisely because it is the thing that unhinged him. Eighty-eight thousand householder-graduates whom Yudhiṣṭhira maintains, each with thirty maids. Another ten thousand eating from gold plates in his house daily. Kāmboja sending antelope-skins and dark-red woolen cloaks. Chariots and cattle and horses by hundreds and thousands, and three thousand camels and she-asses walking about. Brahmins of Vāṭadhāna waiting at the gate with a tribute of three billion, turned away because the ration of golden waterpots was full. And the wonder that got into him worst (2.45.30–31):

> pūrṇe śatasahasre tu viprāṇāṃ pariviṣyatām
> sthāpitā tatra saṃjñābhūc chaṅkho dhmāyati nityaśaḥ
> muhur muhuḥ praṇadatas tasya śaṅkhasya bhārata
> uttamaṃ śabdam aśrauṣaṃ tato romāṇi me 'hṛṣan

*"Whenever a full hundred thousand brahmins had been served, a signal had been set there: a conch is blown. Again and again that conch sounded, and I heard that supreme sound — and then the hairs on my body stood up."*

He ends where he began, in fire: *"Seeing that highest fortune of the son of Pāṇḍu, I do not come to any peace, with my mind burning."*

Śakuni names the remedy for the second time. Duryodhana asks his father for permission. And the old king says the sane thing — *"Vidura is my counsellor and I stand under his instruction; I will meet with him and find out what should be decided in this matter, for he keeps dharma in front and sees far"* — and Duryodhana answers it with the only argument he ever needs (2.45.43–44):

> nivartayiṣyati tvāsau yadi kṣattā sameṣyati
> nivṛtte tvayi rājendra mariṣye 'ham asaṃśayam
> sa mayi tvaṃ mṛte rājan vidureṇa sukhī bhava
> bhokṣyase pṛthivīṃ kṛtsnāṃ kiṃ mayā tvaṃ kariṣyasi

*"That steward will turn you back if he comes. And if you turn back, lord of kings, I shall die — no doubt of it. When I am dead, king, be happy with Vidura; you will enjoy the whole earth. What use will you have for me?"*

The father folds instantly. He orders the builders: a great hall of a thousand pillars and a hundred doors, beautiful, to be made quickly; strew it with jewels, lay out dice, make it well-made and easy to enter, and report to me quietly when it is done. The narrator states the arithmetic without ornament (2.45.48–49):

> duryodhanasya śāntyartham iti niścitya bhūmipaḥ
> …
> apṛṣṭvā viduraṃ hy asya nāsīt kaś cid viniścayaḥ
> dyūtadoṣāṃś ca jānan sa putrasnehād akṛṣyata

*"Having decided that it was for Duryodhana's peace of mind, the lord of the earth … — and he never made any decision without asking Vidura; and, knowing the evils of dicing, he was dragged along by love for his son."*

Vidura comes at a run, hearing that the door of Kali has opened. He bows to his elder brother's feet and says (2.45.52):

> nābhinandāmi te rājan vyavasāyam imaṃ prabho
> putrair bhedo yathā na syād dyūtahetos tathā kuru

*"I do not welcome this resolve of yours, my lord. Act so that there is no split among your sons on account of dicing."*

And Dhṛtarāṣṭra answers with the sentence he will repeat, in some form, until Kurukṣetra is over (2.45.53–57):

> kṣattaḥ putreṣu putrair me kalaho na bhaviṣyati
> divi devāḥ prasādaṃ naḥ kariṣyanti na saṃśayaḥ
> aśubhaṃ vā śubhaṃ vāpi hitaṃ vā yadi vāhitam
> pravartatāṃ suhṛddyūtaṃ diṣṭam etan na saṃśayaḥ
> …
> na vāryo vyavasāyo me viduraitad bravīmi te
> daivam eva paraṃ manye yenaitad upapadyate

*"Steward, there will be no quarrel among my sons. The gods in heaven will be gracious to us; no doubt of it. Whether it is inauspicious or auspicious, whether it is good for us or bad for us — let this friendly game go forward. This is what has been apportioned; no doubt of it. … My resolve is not to be obstructed, Vidura; I tell you this. I hold daiva to be supreme, since it is by daiva that this comes about."*

The order of the reasoning is the whole indictment. He decides; then he declares the decision to be fate; then he forbids anyone to obstruct fate.

When Śakuni's second, more open pitch comes at 2.51 — *"I will take that fortune away by dicing; let the other man be summoned; without going into danger and without fighting at the head of an army, throwing dice, unwounded, a knowing man beats men who do not know"* — Duryodhana adds the argument that finally works, because it is dressed in scripture (2.51.12–13):

> dyūte purāṇair vyavahāraḥ praṇītas; tatrātyayo nāsti na saṃprahāraḥ
> …
> svargadvāraṃ dīvyatāṃ no viśiṣṭaṃ; tadvartināṃ cāpi tathaiva yuktam

*"In dicing, the practice was laid down by the ancients; there is no transgression in it, and no armed clash. … The door of heaven is a distinguished one for those who play, and equally fitting for those who follow the practice."*

Dhṛtarāṣṭra's last words before he gives the order are a prophecy he does nothing about (2.51.14–15):

> vākyaṃ na me rocate yat tvayoktaṃ; yat te priyaṃ tat kriyatāṃ narendra
> paścāt tapsyase tad upākramya vākyaṃ; na hīdṛśaṃ bhāvi vaco hi dharmyam
> dṛṣṭaṃ hy etad vidureṇaivam eva; sarvaṃ pūrvaṃ buddhividyānugena
> tad evaitad avaśasyābhyupaiti; mahad bhayaṃ kṣatriyabījaghāti

*"The words you have spoken do not please me. Let what pleases you be done, lord of men. Having undertaken that word, you will burn for it afterward — a coming word like this cannot be in accord with dharma. All this was foreseen exactly so by Vidura, who follows intelligence and learning. And now it comes upon me, who am helpless: the great terror, the killer of the seed of warriors."*

> evam uktvā dhṛtarāṣṭro manīṣī; daivaṃ matvā paramaṃ dustaraṃ ca
> śaśāsoccaiḥ puruṣān putravākye; sthito rājā daivasaṃmūḍhacetāḥ

*"Having said this, the thoughtful Dhṛtarāṣṭra — holding daiva to be supreme and impossible to cross — loudly gave the order to his men: a king standing on his son's word, his mind deluded by daiva."*

The hall was built in a short time. Then he sent for Vidura and told him to go and fetch Yudhiṣṭhira. Vidura refused once more — *"I am afraid of the destruction of the family"* — and was overruled with the same formula: *dhātrā tu diṣṭasya vaśe kiledaṃ sarvaṃ jagac ceṣṭati na svatantram*, "this whole world moves in the power of what the Ordainer has apportioned; it is not its own master."

## IV. THE SUMMONS

**Sabhā Parva 2.52.1–37** — Vidura, who has argued against this three times, is made the messenger.

> tataḥ prāyād viduro 'śvair udārair; mahājavair balibhiḥ sādhudāntaiḥ
> balān niyukto dhṛtarāṣṭreṇa rājñā; manīṣiṇāṃ pāṇḍavānāṃ sakāśam

*"Then Vidura set out with noble horses, very swift, strong, well broken — commissioned by force by king Dhṛtarāṣṭra — into the presence of the thoughtful Pāṇḍavas."*

*Balān niyuktaḥ.* Ordered under compulsion. He goes anyway, and he is honored on arrival by the brahmins of the city, and comes to Yudhiṣṭhira in a palace like Kubera's. Yudhiṣṭhira receives him properly and asks after his uncle and his cousins — and then, in the same breath, notices something (2.52.5):

> vijñāyate te manaso na praharṣaḥ; kaccit kṣattaḥ kuśalenāgato 'si

*"There is no gladness of mind detectable in you. Steward, have you come here in well-being?"*

Vidura answers first as an ambassador, in the formal register: the great king is well with his sons, sitting surrounded by kinsmen like Indras, pleased with his disciplined sons, free of sorrow, delighting in himself, firm of soul. Then he delivers the message he does not believe (2.52.7–9):

> idaṃ tu tvāṃ kururājo 'bhyuvāca; pūrvaṃ pṛṣṭvā kuśalaṃ cāvyayaṃ ca
> iyaṃ sabhā tvatsabhātulyarūpā; bhrātṝṇāṃ te paśya tām etya putra
> samāgamya bhrātṛbhiḥ pārtha tasyāṃ; suhṛddyūtaṃ kriyatāṃ ramyatāṃ ca
> prīyāmahe bhavataḥ saṃgamena; samāgatāḥ kuravaś caiva sarve
> durodarā vihitā ye tu tatra; mahātmanā dhṛtarāṣṭreṇa rājñā
> tān drakṣyase kitavān saṃniviṣṭān; ity āgato 'haṃ nṛpate taj juṣasva

*"This is what the Kuru king said to you, having first asked after your health and your welfare: 'This hall of your brothers' is equal in form to your own hall. Come, son, and look at it. Meeting with your brothers, Pṛthā's son, let a friendly game be played there, and let there be pleasure. We shall be delighted at your coming, and all the Kurus are gathered.' And the gamblers appointed there by the great king Dhṛtarāṣṭra — you will see them, seated. That is why I have come, king. Take pleasure in it."*

Notice what is inside the invitation: *the gamblers have already been appointed and seated*. Nothing is hidden. Yudhiṣṭhira's reply is immediate and correct (2.52.10):

> dyūte kṣattaḥ kalaho vidyate naḥ; ko vai dyūtaṃ rocayed budhyamānaḥ
> kiṃ vā bhavān manyate yuktarūpaṃ; bhavadvākye sarva eva sthitāḥ sma

*"Steward, in dicing there is quarrelling for us. What man, if he were thinking, would want to dice? What do you consider the fitting thing here? We all stand on your word."*

He has asked Vidura to decide. And Vidura, who has been sent under compulsion, gives him the most honest answer available to a man in that position and no more (2.52.11):

> jānāmy ahaṃ dyūtam anarthamūlaṃ; kṛtaś ca yatno 'sya mayā nivāraṇe
> rājā tu māṃ prāhiṇot tvatsakāśaṃ; śrutvā vidvañ śreya ihācarasva

*"I know that dicing is the root of ruin, and effort was made by me to prevent it. The king sent me to you. Having heard, learned one, do here what is better."*

Yudhiṣṭhira then asks the question a man asks when he already knows: *who else is playing?* Vidura names them: Śakuni, king of Gāndhāra — *rājātidevī kṛtahasto matākṣaḥ*, "a king who plays excessively, with a made hand, an expert with dice" — and Viviṃśati and king Citrasena, Satyavrata, Purumitra, Jaya.

And Yudhiṣṭhira sees it whole, and goes (2.52.14–16):

> mahābhayāḥ kitavāḥ saṃniviṣṭā; māyopadhā devitāro 'tra santi
> dhātrā tu diṣṭasya vaśe kiledaṃ; nādevanaṃ kitavair adya tair me
> nāhaṃ rājño dhṛtarāṣṭrasya śāsanān; na gantum icchāmi kave durodaram
> iṣṭo hi putrasya pitā sadaiva; tad asmi kartā vidurāttha māṃ yathā
> na cākāmaḥ śakuninā devitāhaṃ; na cen māṃ dhṛṣṇur āhvayitā sabhāyām
> āhūto 'haṃ na nivarte kadā cit; tad āhitaṃ śāśvataṃ vai vrataṃ me

*"Very dangerous gamblers are seated there; there are players there whose foundation is trickery. But this whole world moves under the power of what the Ordainer has apportioned — so there is no not-playing with those gamblers for me today. I do not wish to go to the dice, wise one, by reason of king Dhṛtarāṣṭra's command — a father is always to be desired by a son. And I will not play with Śakuni unwillingly, unless the bold man challenges me in the assembly: summoned, I never turn back. That is the vow laid on me, and it is permanent."*

Watch the movement of that speech. The clear-eyed appraisal — *these are cheats* — then daiva, invoked in the same words Dhṛtarāṣṭra used, converting the appraisal into a foregone conclusion; then the insistence that he goes not because he was ordered but because *a father is always to be desired*; then the vow, the only part that is not a rationalization, stated in a conditional so precise it is nearly a prayer: *unless he challenges me, I need not play.* He knows he will be challenged.

Then he stops arguing and starts packing. He orders everything for the journey and sets out the next day with his retinue, his household, and the women, *draupadīm ādikṛtvā* — "putting Draupadī at their head." And the narrator, who has watched this, cannot let it pass without a comment of his own (2.52.18):

> daivaṃ prajñāṃ tu muṣṇāti tejaś cakṣur ivāpatat
> dhātuś ca vaśam anveti pāśair iva naraḥ sitaḥ

*"Daiva steals a man's discernment as a glare steals the eye, and a man goes along under the Ordainer's power like one bound with cords."*

Then: *"Saying this, king Yudhiṣṭhira set out with the steward — Pṛthā's son, the tamer of foes, not able to bear that summons."* He rode in a chariot given him by Bāhlīka, and went out in state, blazing with royal fortune, with brahmins going before him — *"summoned by Dhṛtarāṣṭra, and by the coming-round of time."*

At Hāstinapura the courtesies are exact and complete, and the poem's tenderness toward them is what makes them terrible. He met his uncle. He met Droṇa, Bhīṣma, Karṇa, Kṛpa, Aśvatthāman; Somadatta, Duryodhana, Śalya, Śakuni, Jayadratha, and the other kings already gathered. Surrounded by all his brothers he entered the house, and there he saw Gāndhārī, devoted to her husband, surrounded by her daughters-in-law like Rohiṇī by the stars. He greeted her and she greeted him back. He saw his old father, the lord whose eye is his intelligence. The king smelled the heads of the four younger Pāṇḍavas, Bhīmasena at their head. And joy arose among the Kurus at the sight of these tigers among men, lovely to look at.

Only one thing snags (2.52.31–32):

> viviśus te 'bhyanujñātā ratnavanti gṛhāṇy atha
> dadṛśuś copayātās tān draupadīpramukhāḥ striyaḥ
> yājñasenyāḥ parām ṛddhiṃ dṛṣṭvā prajvalitām iva
> snuṣās tā dhṛtarāṣṭrasya nātipramanaso 'bhavan

*"Given leave, they entered the jewel-filled apartments; and the women, Draupadī at their head, came out and saw them. Seeing the supreme prosperity of Yajñasena's daughter, all but blazing, the daughters-in-law of Dhṛtarāṣṭra were not very glad in their minds."*

The same word, and the same all-but-blazing — *prajvalitām iva*, which was *jvalantīm iva* when Duryodhana said it on the road. The men's grievance has an exact counterpart in the women's quarters, and the poem records it in a single verse and moves on.

They bathed and did their exercises and were rubbed with divine sandal-paste; they had the brahmins pronounce the blessing; they ate agreeable food and went to their rooms and were sung to sleep by women. That night passed for them pleasantly, praised and rested; in due time they gave up sleep. And in the morning, having done the morning rites, well rested (2.52.37):

> sabhāṃ ramyāṃ praviviśuḥ kitavair abhisaṃvṛtām

*"— they entered the beautiful hall, which was crowded with gamblers."*

## V. THE GAME

**Sabhā Parva 2.53.1–2.58.43** — the stakes, in order.

Śakuni opens: *"The hall is spread, king; these men are ready for the pleasure. Let the dice be cast and let the terms of the play be settled, Yudhiṣṭhira."*

Yudhiṣṭhira objects first on principle, and it is important that he does (2.53.2–3):

> nikṛtir devanaṃ pāpaṃ na kṣātro 'tra parākramaḥ
> na ca nītir dhruvā rājan kiṃ tvaṃ dyūtaṃ praśaṃsasi
> na hi mānaṃ praśaṃsanti nikṛtau kitavasya ha
> śakune maiva no jaiṣīr amārgeṇa nṛśaṃsavat

*"Play is trickery, and evil; there is no warrior's valor in it, and no settled policy. Why do you praise dicing? Men do not praise a gambler's honor when there is fraud in it. Śakuni — do not beat us that way, off the road, cruelly."*

Śakuni answers with a piece of sophistry so clean it should be studied (2.53.11–12):

> śrotriyo 'śrotriyam uta nikṛtyaiva yudhiṣṭhira
> vidvān aviduṣo 'bhyeti nāhus tāṃ nikṛtiṃ janāḥ
> evaṃ tvaṃ mām ihābhyetya nikṛtiṃ yadi manyase
> devanād vinivartasva yadi te vidyate bhayam

*"A learned man approaches an unlearned man by exactly that trickery, Yudhiṣṭhira; a man who knows approaches men who do not know — and people do not call that fraud. If, having come to me here, you consider it fraud, then turn back from the game — if you are afraid."*

Every skill is an advantage over the unskilled; therefore skill is not cheating; therefore your objection is cowardice. It is not a good argument. It works, because of what it is aimed at (2.53.13):

> āhūto na nivarteyam iti me vratam āhitam
> vidhiś ca balavān rājan diṣṭasyāsmi vaśe sthitaḥ

*"'Summoned, I do not turn back' — that is the vow laid on me. And Ordinance is strong, king: I stand in the power of what is apportioned."*

There is one more procedural objection, and it is the last piece of resistance he offers. Duryodhana announces that *he* will supply the wealth and Śakuni will play on his behalf; Yudhiṣṭhira says (2.53.16):

> anyenānyasya viṣamaṃ devanaṃ pratibhāti me
> etad vidvann upādatsva kāmam evaṃ pravartatām

*"For one man to play with another man's stake seems to me irregular. You know this — take it up if you like; let it proceed even so."*

He names the irregularity and then permits it in the same verse. Then the kings enter with Dhṛtarāṣṭra at their head; Bhīṣma, Droṇa, Kṛpa and Vidura follow them *nātīvaprītamanasaḥ*, "not at all glad in their minds"; the assembly shines like heaven with gods in it; and *prāvartata mahārāja suhṛddyūtam anantaram* — "and then, great king, the friendly game began."

What follows is one long formula, repeated with almost no variation, and its monotony is the instrument. Yudhiṣṭhira describes a thing in loving detail; he says *etad rājan dhanaṃ mahyaṃ tena dīvyāmy ahaṃ tvayā*, "this, king, is my wealth; with it I play against you"; and then, verbatim, ten times and more:

> etac chrutvā vyavasito nikṛtiṃ samupāśritaḥ
> jitam ity eva śakunir yudhiṣṭhiram abhāṣata

*"Hearing that, the determined one, who had taken his stand on fraud, said just this to Yudhiṣṭhira: 'Won.'"*

**First**, a jewel out of the churning of the ocean, a splendid necklace with gold for its finest ornament. *Won.*

**Second**, a hundred jars each filled with a thousand *niṣka*s of gold; an inexhaustible treasury of wrought gold. *Won.*

**Third**, the chariot: tiger-skinned, sound of wheel and frame, hung with a net of little bells, thunder-sounding, that carried them here, drawn by eight horses the color of ospreys, so admired throughout the kingdom that *"of these it cannot be said that their feet touch the ground."* *Won.*

**Fourth**, a thousand rutting elephants with golden girths and golden garlands, tusks like chariot-poles, each with eight cow-elephants, all of them breakers of cities, dark as mountains and as rainclouds. *Won.*

**Fifth**, a hundred thousand serving-women, young, wearing shell bracelets and armlets and neck-ornaments, scented with sandal, skilled in dance and song. *Won.*

**Sixth**, the same number of male servants — wise, intelligent, capable, young, wearing polished earrings, plate in hand, feeding guests day and night. *Won.*

**Seventh**, the same number of chariots with gold trappings and banners, with trained horses and warriors who fight with skill, each man drawing a thousand a month whether he fights or not. *Won.*

**Eighth**, the horses speckled like partridges, gold-garlanded, that Citraratha gave in gladness to the wielder of Gāṇḍīva. *Won.*

**Ninth**, ten thousand carts and chariots and horses; and sixty thousand men picked out class by class, broad-chested, standing about drinking milk and eating fine rice. *Won.*

**Tenth**, four hundred treasure-chests bound with copper and iron, each holding five *droṇa*s of refined gold. *Won.*

Then Śakuni changes the register (2.58.1):

> bahu vittaṃ parājaiṣīḥ pāṇḍavānāṃ yudhiṣṭhira
> ācakṣva vittaṃ kaunteya yadi te 'sty aparājitam

*"You have gambled away much of the Pāṇḍavas' wealth, Yudhiṣṭhira. Declare your wealth, son of Kuntī, if you have any that is not yet lost."*

And Yudhiṣṭhira, stung, answers with numbers instead of things — *ayuta, prayuta, kharva, padma, arbuda, śaṅkha, nikharva, samudra*: ten thousand, a million, and on up through the named orders of magnitude to "an ocean." *Won.* Then all the cattle and horses and milk-cows and goats and sheep, uncountable, every one belonging to every class as far as the Sindhu. *Won.* Then (2.58.7):

> puraṃ janapado bhūmir abrāhmaṇadhanaiḥ saha
> abrāhmaṇāś ca puruṣā rājañ śiṣṭaṃ dhanaṃ mama

*"The city, the countryside, the land, together with the property of all who are not brahmins, and the men who are not brahmins — king, that is the wealth left to me."* *Won.*

The kingdom is gone. He has kept back the brahmins and their property, and nothing else. Then (2.58.9):

> rājaputrā ime rājañ śobhante yena bhūṣitāḥ
> kuṇḍalāni ca niṣkāś ca sarvaṃ cāṅgavibhūṣaṇam

*"The ornaments by which these princes here are made splendid — the earrings, the gold pieces, every body-ornament."* *Won.*

He has now stripped his brothers where they sit. And then, with nothing between him and them (2.58.11):

> śyāmo yuvā lohitākṣaḥ siṃhaskandho mahābhujaḥ
> nakulo glaha eko me yac caitat svagataṃ dhanam

*"Dark, young, red-eyed, lion-shouldered, great-armed — Nakula is my single throw; that is the wealth that belongs to me."*

Śakuni's reply is the first cruelty spoken aloud in the hall: *"Nakula is dear to you, prince Yudhiṣṭhira. He has come into our possession. With what will you play now?"*

> ayaṃ dharmān sahadevo 'nuśāsti; loke hy asmin paṇḍitākhyāṃ gataś ca
> anarhatā rājaputreṇa tena; tvayā dīvyāmy apriyavat priyeṇa

*"This Sahadeva teaches the dharmas; in this world he has gone by the name of scholar. With that prince, who does not deserve it, dear to me as though he were not dear — with him I play against you."* *Won.*

Śakuni now does something no rule requires. He pauses to point (2.58.16):

> mādrīputrau priyau rājaṃs tavemau vijitau mayā
> garīyāṃsau tu te manye bhīmasenadhanaṃjayau

*"These two sons of Mādrī, dear to you, king, have been won by me. But I think Bhīmasena and Arjuna are weightier to you."*

He is telling him, out loud, that he knows the two he has taken are the two the man loves least. Yudhiṣṭhira hears it exactly (2.58.17):

> adharmaṃ carase nūnaṃ yo nāvekṣasi vai nayam
> yo naḥ sumanasāṃ mūḍha vibhedaṃ kartum icchasi

*"You are certainly practicing adharma, you who pay no attention to right conduct — you who want to make a split among us, fool, when our minds are good toward each other."*

Śakuni's answer is a proverb and a bow, and it is contemptuous: *"A drunk man falls in a hole; a careless man runs into a post. You are the elder, king; you are the better man. Homage to you, bull of the Bharatas."*

Then Arjuna: *"He who is our ferryman across the battle as a ship across water, the swift prince, conqueror of enemies — with him, who does not deserve it, the hero of the world, with Phalguna I play, Śakuni."* *Won.*

Then Śakuni, again unprompted: *"I have won the bowman of the Pāṇḍavas, the left-handed archer. Play now with Bhīma, whom you hold dear, king — with what remains to you of a stake."*

Then Bhīma (2.58.23–24):

> yo no netā yo yudhāṃ naḥ praṇetā; yathā vajrī dānavaśatrur ekaḥ
> tiryakprekṣī saṃhatabhrūr mahātmā; siṃhaskandho yaś ca sadātyamarṣī
> balena tulyo yasya pumān na vidyate; gadābhṛtām agrya ihārimardanaḥ
> anarhatā rājaputreṇa tena; dīvyāmy ahaṃ bhīmasenena rājan

*"He who is our leader, who leads us in battle, like the wielder of the thunderbolt, sole enemy of the demons; who looks sidelong with his brows knit together, the great-souled one, lion-shouldered, who is always past bearing anger; the man whose equal in strength does not exist, first of mace-bearers, crusher of enemies here — with that prince, who does not deserve it, with Bhīmasena I play, king."* *Won.*

Śakuni, a third time: *"You have gambled away much wealth, and your brothers with their horses and elephants. Declare your wealth, son of Kuntī, if you have any that is not yet lost."*

> ahaṃ viśiṣṭaḥ sarveṣāṃ bhrātṝṇāṃ dayitas tathā
> kuryāmas te jitāḥ karma svayam ātmany upaplave

*"I am the distinguished one of all my brothers, and the beloved one. Won, we would do your work — with a calamity upon my own self."* *Won.*

Now Śakuni delivers a piece of moral instruction (2.58.29):

> etat pāpiṣṭham akaror yad ātmānaṃ parājitaḥ
> śiṣṭe sati dhane rājan pāpa ātmaparājayaḥ

*"You have done the most evil thing there is, in that you have gambled yourself away. When wealth is still left, king, the losing of one's own self is an evil act."*

He is *correct* — losing oneself while property remains is a recognized wrong. He states the rule at the exact moment stating it is useful, having spent the whole game producing the condition he now condemns. And then he supplies the wealth that is still left (2.58.31):

> asti vai te priyā devī glaha eko 'parājitaḥ
> paṇasva kṛṣṇāṃ pāñcālīṃ tayātmānaṃ punar jaya

*"You do have a beloved queen — one throw not yet lost. Stake Kṛṣṇā of Pāñcāla; with her, win yourself back."*

The offer is designed to sound like rescue. And Yudhiṣṭhira, who is now a slave being told how to become free, describes his wife for six verses to a room full of men (2.58.32–37):

> naiva hrasvā na mahatī nātikṛṣṇā na rohiṇī
> sarāgaraktanetrā ca tayā dīvyāmy ahaṃ tvayā
> śāradotpalapatrākṣyā śāradotpalagandhayā
> śāradotpalasevinyā rūpeṇa śrīsamānayā
> tathaiva syād ānṛśaṃsyāt tathā syād rūpasaṃpadā
> tathā syāc chīlasaṃpattyā yām icchet puruṣaḥ striyam
> caramaṃ saṃviśati yā prathamaṃ pratibudhyate
> ā gopālāvipālebhyaḥ sarvaṃ veda kṛtākṛtam

*"Not short and not tall, not too dark and not ruddy, her eyes reddened with passion — with her I play against you. Her eyes are the petals of the autumn lotus; her fragrance is the autumn lotus; she is the attendant of the autumn lotus; in beauty she is the equal of Śrī. Such a woman a man would wish for, in her freedom from cruelty, in her perfection of form, in her perfection of character. She is the last to lie down and the first to wake. Down to the cowherds and the shepherds, she knows everything that has been done and not done."*

He praises her *as a stake* — beauty, kindness, competence at running the household — in the same syntax he used for the elephants. And the comparison to Śrī is the *aṃśāvataraṇa*'s own description: he is staking Fortune herself.

> tayaivaṃvidhayā rājan pāñcālyāhaṃ sumadhyayā
> glahaṃ dīvyāmi cārvaṅgyā draupadyā hanta saubala

*"With her, of such a kind, king — with the Pāñcāla woman, slender-waisted, beautiful of limb, with Draupadī — I play the throw. There, Saubala."*

And the hall breaks (2.58.38–43):

> evam ukte tu vacane dharmarājena bhārata
> dhig dhig ity eva vṛddhānāṃ sabhyānāṃ niḥsṛtā giraḥ
> cukṣubhe sā sabhā rājan rājñāṃ saṃjajñire kathāḥ
> bhīṣmadroṇakṛpādīnāṃ svedaś ca samajāyata
> śiro gṛhītvā viduro gatasattva ivābhavat
> āste dhyāyann adhovaktro niḥśvasan pannago yathā
> dhṛtarāṣṭras tu saṃhṛṣṭaḥ paryapṛcchat punaḥ punaḥ
> kiṃ jitaṃ kiṃ jitam iti hy ākāraṃ nābhyarakṣata
> jaharṣa karṇo 'tibhṛśaṃ saha duḥśāsanādibhiḥ
> itareṣāṃ tu sabhyānāṃ netrebhyaḥ prāpataj jalam

*"When that word had been spoken by the Dharma King, cries of 'Shame! Shame!' broke from the elders in the assembly. The hall shook; talk started up among the kings; sweat came out on Bhīṣma, Droṇa, Kṛpa and the rest. Vidura took hold of his head and became like a man whose strength has gone out of him; he sat brooding, face down, hissing like a snake. But Dhṛtarāṣṭra, thrilled, kept asking over and over, 'What is won? What is won?' — he could not keep his composure. Karṇa was delighted beyond measure, and Duḥśāsana and the others with him. But from the eyes of the rest of the assembly, water fell."*

> saubalas tv avicāryaiva jitakāśī madotkaṭaḥ
> jitam ity eva tān akṣān punar evānvapadyata

*"And Saubala, without any deliberation at all, wearing the look of a winner, swollen with intoxication, took up those dice again with just this: 'Won.'"*

## VI. THE HALL

**Sabhā Parva 2.59.1–2.63.36** — from Duryodhana's order through Draupadī's question to the granting of the boons.

Duryodhana does not wait a verse (2.59.1):

> ehi kṣattar draupadīm ānayasva; priyāṃ bhāryāṃ saṃmatāṃ pāṇḍavānām
> saṃmārjatāṃ veśma paraitu śīghram; ānando naḥ saha dāsībhir astu

*"Come, steward, bring Draupadī — the beloved wife, honored by the Pāṇḍavas. Let her sweep the house; let her go quickly to the other women; let there be pleasure for us, with the serving-maids."*

Vidura refuses out loud and in public, and — this matters — he refuses on a point of law (2.59.4):

> na hi dāsītvam āpannā kṛṣṇā bhavati bhārata
> anīśena hi rājñaiṣā paṇe nyasteti me matiḥ

*"Kṛṣṇā has not come into slavery, Bhārata. My judgment is that she was put down as a stake by a king who was no longer her master."*

The argument that Draupadī will make from the floor has already been made by the wisest man in the room, before she is brought in. He goes on: *"You bear fruit like a bamboo, killing yourself in the bearing … A man should not be one who cuts to the quick, nor speak cruelly, nor take what is another's by base means. You do not know it, son of Dhṛtarāṣṭra: this is the crooked and terrible doorway of hell, and many Kurus are going in after you, with Duḥśāsana."* And, finally: *"Gourds sink and stones float; boats founder in water again and again. The king's son is a fool and does not hear my words, which are wholesome. Surely this will be the end of the Kurus — a very cruel, all-carrying-away destruction."*

Duryodhana says *"to hell with the steward"* and turns to a servant.

The messenger goes and finds her and delivers the sentence flatly: *"Yudhiṣṭhira, drunk with the madness of dicing, has been beaten — Duryodhana has won you, Draupadī. Come to Dhṛtarāṣṭra's house; I am taking you to work, daughter of Yajñasena."*

And she does not weep, and does not appeal. She finds the flaw (2.60.5–7):

> kathaṃ tv evaṃ vadasi prātikāmin; ko vai dīvyed bhāryayā rājaputraḥ
> mūḍho rājā dyūtamadena matta; āho nānyat kaitavam asya kiṃ cit
> …
> gaccha tvaṃ kitavaṃ gatvā sabhāyāṃ pṛccha sūtaja
> kiṃ nu pūrvaṃ parājaiṣīr ātmānaṃ māṃ nu bhārata
> etaj jñātvā tvam āgaccha tato māṃ naya sūtaja

*"How can you say such a thing, messenger? What prince would play with his wife? Was the king so deluded, so drunk with the madness of dice — was there nothing else left to him to stake? … Go. Go to the gambler in the hall and ask him, charioteer's son: which did you lose first, yourself or me, Bhārata? Come back when you know that; then take me, charioteer's son."*

The messenger asks it in open court: *"Whose lord were you when you lost us? — that is what Draupadī asks you. Which did you lose first, yourself, or else me?"*

> yudhiṣṭhiras tu niśceṣṭo gatasattva ivābhavat
> na taṃ sūtaṃ pratyuvāca vacanaṃ sādhv asādhu vā

*"But Yudhiṣṭhira, motionless, became like a man whose strength has gone out of him. He answered the charioteer's son not a word, good or bad."*

He never answers it. Not in this scene, not in the next, not at any point in the Sabhā Parva. The most articulate man in the epic, whose whole authority is that he can say what dharma is, is asked one question by his wife and does not speak.

Duryodhana orders her brought into the hall to ask it in person. The messenger goes back, shaken, and says something extraordinary for a servant to say (2.60.12):

> sabhyās tv amī rājaputry āhvayanti; manye prāptaḥ saṃkṣayaḥ kauravāṇām
> na vai samṛddhiṃ pālayate laghīyān; yat tvaṃ sabhām eṣyasi rājaputri

*"The assembly is calling you, princess. I think the destruction of the Kauravas has arrived. A lesser man does not protect his own prosperity — since you, a princess, are to come into the hall."*

And her answer names the mechanism the entire epic runs on (2.60.13):

> evaṃ nūnaṃ vyadadhāt saṃvidhātā; sparśāv ubhau spṛśato dhīrabālau
> dharmaṃ tv ekaṃ paramaṃ prāha loke; sa naḥ śamaṃ dhāsyati gopyamānaḥ

*"Surely the Ordainer has arranged it so: touch strikes both the wise man and the child. But he has declared dharma alone supreme in this world; kept guarded, it will set us at peace."*

*Gopyamānaḥ* — "being guarded." Dharma protects those who protect it. She is about to find out what happens when nobody does.

Duḥśāsana is sent when the servant will not go again.

> ehy ehi pāñcāli jitāsi kṛṣṇe; duryodhanaṃ paśya vimuktalajjā
> kurūn bhajasvāyatapadmanetre; dharmeṇa labdhāsi sabhāṃ paraihi

*"Come, come, Pāñcālī; you have been won, Kṛṣṇā. Look at Duryodhana, and let your shame go. Long-lotus-eyed one, take to the Kurus. You have been acquired by dharma. Go to the hall."*

*Dharmeṇa labdhā.* Acquired lawfully. It is the phrase they will keep using.

> tataḥ samutthāya sudurmanāḥ sā; vivarṇam āmṛjya mukhaṃ kareṇa
> ārtā pradudrāva yataḥ striyas tā; vṛddhasya rājñaḥ kurupuṃgavasya
> tato javenābhisasāra roṣād; duḥśāsanas tām abhigarjamānaḥ
> dīrgheṣu nīleṣv atha cormimatsu; jagrāha keśeṣu narendrapatnīm

*"Then she stood up, sick at heart, wiped her bloodless face with her hand, and in her distress ran toward the women of the old king, the bull of the Kurus. Then Duḥśāsana rushed at her at speed, in fury, roaring at her, and seized the wife of a lord of men by the hair — long, dark, and waved."*

The poem stops to identify the hair (2.60.23):

> ye rājasūyāvabhṛthe jalena; mahākratau mantrapūtena siktāḥ
> te pāṇḍavānāṃ paribhūya vīryaṃ; balāt pramṛṣṭā dhṛtarāṣṭrajena

*"Those locks, which at the final bath of the Rājasūya, at that great rite, had been sprinkled with water purified by mantra — those, in contempt of the Pāṇḍavas' valor, were mauled by force by the son of Dhṛtarāṣṭra."*

The hair that was consecrated at the imperial sacrifice is the handle he uses. The desecration is precise and the poem wants us to know it is precise.

> sa tāṃ parāmṛśya sabhāsamīpam; ānīya kṛṣṇām atikṛṣṇakeśīm
> duḥśāsano nāthavatīm anāthavac; cakarṣa vāyuḥ kadalīm ivārtām

*"Having laid hands on her, Duḥśāsana brought Kṛṣṇā of the very dark hair up to the hall and dragged her — a woman who had protectors, as though she had none — the way the wind drags a plantain tree that suffers it."*

*Nāthavatīm anāthavat.* She has five husbands, and she is being handled like a woman with nobody.

She says quietly, being dragged, her body bent: *"Today I am in my season, and I have one garment on, dull-witted man; you should not take me to the hall, unworthy man."* And he answers (2.60.26–27):

> kṛṣṇaṃ ca jiṣṇuṃ ca hariṃ naraṃ ca; trāṇāya vikrośa nayāmi hi tvām
> rajasvalā vā bhava yājñaseni; ekāmbarā vāpy atha vā vivastrā
> dyūte jitā cāsi kṛtāsi dāsī; dāsīṣu kāmaś ca yathopajoṣam

*"Cry out to Kṛṣṇa and Jiṣṇu and Hari and Nara for rescue — I am taking you all the same. Be in your season, daughter of Yajñasena, or in one garment, or with no garment at all: you have been won at dice, and made a slave; and with slave women, desire goes as it pleases."*

Hair scattered, half her garment fallen, shaken by Duḥśāsana, burning with shame and with a rage she cannot act on, she speaks — and what she says first is not about herself (2.60.29–34):

> ime sabhāyām upadiṣṭaśāstrāḥ; kriyāvantaḥ sarva evendrakalpāḥ
> gurusthānā guravaś caiva sarve; teṣām agre notsahe sthātum evam
> nṛśaṃsakarmaṃs tvam anāryavṛtta; mā māṃ vivastrāṃ kṛdhi mā vikārṣīḥ
> na marṣayeyus tava rājaputrāḥ; sendrāpi devā yadi te sahāyāḥ
> dharme sthito dharmasutaś ca rājā; dharmaś ca sūkṣmo nipuṇopalabhyaḥ
> vācāpi bhartuḥ paramāṇumātraṃ; necchāmi doṣaṃ svaguṇān visṛjya

*"These men in the hall have been taught the treatises; they all perform the rites; they are all like Indra. All of them stand in the place of teachers, and are teachers. Before them I cannot bear to stand like this. Man of cruel acts, of ignoble conduct — do not make me naked, do not drag me. The princes would not endure it from you, not if the gods with Indra were your allies. The king, the son of Dharma, stands in dharma — and dharma is subtle, to be grasped only by the skilful. Even by a word I do not wish to give up my own good qualities and impute a fault to my husband, not so much as an atom's worth."*

*Dharmaś ca sūkṣmaḥ.* Dharma is subtle. She says it in her own defense of her husband; Bhīṣma will say the same words in a minute to explain why he can do nothing. The identical proposition is a shield in her mouth and an excuse in his.

Then she turns on the room (2.60.32–34):

> idaṃ tv anāryaṃ kuruvīramadhye; rajasvalāṃ yat parikarṣase mām
> na cāpi kaś cit kurute 'tra pūjāṃ; dhruvaṃ tavedaṃ matam anvapadyan
> dhig astu naṣṭaḥ khalu bhāratānāṃ; dharmas tathā kṣatravidāṃ ca vṛttam
> yatrābhyatītāṃ kurudharmavelāṃ; prekṣanti sarve kuravaḥ sabhāyām
> droṇasya bhīṣmasya ca nāsti sattvaṃ; dhruvaṃ tathaivāsya mahātmano 'pi

*"But this is ignoble — that in the middle of the Kuru heroes you drag me while I am in my season. And nobody here does anything about it. Surely they have all gone over to your opinion. Shame! The dharma of the Bhāratas is lost, and so is the practice of those who know the warrior's code — for all the Kurus in this hall are watching the boundary-line of Kuru dharma being crossed. Droṇa has no strength in him, and neither has Bhīṣma — nor, surely, has that great-souled one either."*

And then the poem does something quiet and devastating: it tells us where she is looking.

> tathā bruvantī karuṇaṃ sumadhyamā; kākṣeṇa bhartṝn kupitān apaśyat
> sā pāṇḍavān kopaparītadehān; saṃdīpayām āsa kaṭākṣapātaiḥ
> hṛtena rājyena tathā dhanena; ratnaiś ca mukhyair na tathā babhūva
> yathārtayā kopasamīritena; kṛṣṇākaṭākṣeṇa babhūva duḥkham

*"Speaking so, pitiably, the slender-waisted one looked sidelong at her furious husbands. And she set the Pāṇḍavas — whose bodies were already wrapped in rage — on fire, with the falling of those sidelong glances. Not by the kingdom taken, nor by the wealth, nor by the chief jewels, did it become as it then became: the grief that came of Kṛṣṇā's sidelong glance, driven by anguish and by rage."*

Everything they have lost is less than the way she looks at them.

Duḥśāsana sees her looking at her wretched husbands and shakes her violently until she is nearly senseless, laughing, and says the word *dāsī* — slave-woman. Karṇa applauds aloud; Śakuni congratulates Duḥśāsana. Everyone else in the hall is in pain and silent.

Then Bhīṣma answers her question (2.60.40–42):

> na dharmasaukṣmyāt subhage vivaktuṃ; śaknomi te praśnam imaṃ yathāvat
> asvo hy aśaktaḥ paṇituṃ parasvaṃ; striyaś ca bhartur vaśatāṃ samīkṣya
> tyajeta sarvāṃ pṛthivīṃ samṛddhāṃ; yudhiṣṭhiraḥ satyam atho na jahyāt
> uktaṃ jito 'smīti ca pāṇḍavena; tasmān na śaknomi vivektum etat
> dyūte 'dvitīyaḥ śakunir nareṣu; kuntīsutas tena nisṛṣṭakāmaḥ
> na manyate tāṃ nikṛtiṃ mahātmā; tasmān na te praśnam imaṃ bravīmi

*"Because of the subtlety of dharma, fortunate woman, I am not able to decide this question of yours as it should be decided. A man who is not his own cannot stake what belongs to another — and yet, considering that a woman is in her husband's power … Yudhiṣṭhira would abandon the whole prosperous earth and would not abandon truth; and the Pāṇḍava has said 'I am won.' Therefore I cannot decide it. Śakuni has no second among men at dice, and the son of Kuntī gave him his choice of the play; the great-souled one does not consider it fraud. Therefore I do not answer this question of yours."*

Every clause is true and the sum is nothing. He gives the argument for her, gives the argument against, and puts them both down. Draupadī's reply hits the actual flaw (2.60.43–45):

> āhūya rājā kuśalaiḥ sabhāyāṃ; duṣṭātmabhir naikṛtikair anāryaiḥ
> dyūtapriyair nātikṛtaprayatnaḥ; kasmād ayaṃ nāma nisṛṣṭakāmaḥ
> sa śuddhabhāvo nikṛtipravṛttim; abudhyamānaḥ kurupāṇḍavāgryaḥ
> saṃbhūya sarvaiś ca jito 'pi yasmāt; paścāc ca yat kaitavam abhyupetaḥ
> tiṣṭhanti ceme kuravaḥ sabhāyām; īśāḥ sutānāṃ ca tathā snuṣāṇām
> samīkṣya sarve mama cāpi vākyaṃ; vibrūta me praśnam imaṃ yathāvat

*"The king was summoned into the hall by experts — by men of ruined character, cheats, ignoble men, lovers of dicing — and he was not much practiced. How can he be called a man who was given his choice? He is pure in nature and did not understand the operation of the fraud, that first man of the Kurus and Pāṇḍavas; he was beaten by all of them acting together, and only afterward did he come up against the cheating. And these Kurus stand in the hall, masters of their sons and of their sons' wives. Let them all consider — and my words too — and answer this question of mine as it should be answered."*

Nobody does. Bhīma erupts at Yudhiṣṭhira instead (2.61.1–6): *"Gamblers have prostitutes in their houses, Yudhiṣṭhira, and even they do not stake them; even for those there is pity. … The tribute of Kāśi, the vehicles, the wealth, the armor, the weapons, the kingdom, yourself, and us — all of it taken by others through cheating; and I felt no anger at that, for you were master of it all. But this I hold to be gone too far, that Draupadī was staked. … For your sake this girl, who does not deserve it, is being tormented by low, cruel men who love fraud. Because of her this rage falls on you, king. I am going to burn your arms. Sahadeva — bring fire."*

Arjuna stops him: *"You have never spoken words like these before. Surely your reverence for dharma has been destroyed by the cruelty of these enemies. Do not give them what they want; practice the highest dharma. No one should transgress an elder brother who is righteous. The king was summoned by enemies, and remembering the warrior's dharma he played at another's wish; that is a great glory to us."* Bhīma's reply is one line: *"If I did not know that this was so, Arjuna, I would force those two arms of his into a blazing fire."*

Then a Kaurava speaks — Vikarṇa, Duryodhana's own brother (2.61.11–24). He asks the assembly four times to answer the woman's question, and gets nothing: *"Bhīṣma and Dhṛtarāṣṭra, the two oldest of the Kurus, having come together have said nothing; nor has the great-minded Vidura. Bhāradvāja, the teacher of all, and Kṛpa too — those two best of brahmins have not answered the question either."* He grinds his palms together, breathes out, and rules (2.61.20–24):

> catvāry āhur naraśreṣṭhā vyasanāni mahīkṣitām
> mṛgayāṃ pānam akṣāṃś ca grāmye caivātisaktatām
> eteṣu hi naraḥ sakto dharmam utsṛjya vartate
> tathāyuktena ca kṛtāṃ kriyāṃ loko na manyate
> …
> sādhāraṇī ca sarveṣāṃ pāṇḍavānām aninditā
> jitena pūrvaṃ cānena pāṇḍavena kṛtaḥ paṇaḥ
> iyaṃ ca kīrtitā kṛṣṇā saubalena paṇārthinā
> etat sarvaṃ vicāryāhaṃ manye na vijitām imām

*"The best of men say there are four vices of kings: hunting, drink, dice, and excessive attachment to women. A man attached to these acts with dharma thrown aside, and the world does not recognize an act done by a man so joined. … Furthermore, the blameless woman is held in common by all the Pāṇḍavas; and this Pāṇḍava had already been won when he made the stake; and Kṛṣṇā was named by Saubala, who wanted the stake. Weighing all of this, I hold that she has not been won."*

Three arguments, each independently sufficient. A great shout goes up in the hall, praising Vikarṇa and reviling Śakuni. And when it dies down Karṇa answers, and his answer is the true voice of the hall (2.61.28–37):

> ete na kiṃ cid apy āhuś codyamānāpi kṛṣṇayā
> dharmeṇa vijitāṃ manye manyante drupadātmajām
> …
> abhyantarā ca sarvasve draupadī bharatarṣabha
> evaṃ dharmajitāṃ kṛṣṇāṃ manyase na jitāṃ katham
> kīrtitā draupadī vācā anujñātā ca pāṇḍavaiḥ
> bhavaty avijitā kena hetunaiṣā matā tava
> …
> eko bhartā striyā devair vihitaḥ kurunandana
> iyaṃ tv anekavaśagā bandhakīti viniścitā
> asyāḥ sabhām ānayanaṃ na citram iti me matiḥ
> ekāmbaradharatvaṃ vāpy atha vāpi vivastratā

*"These men have said nothing at all, though pressed by Kṛṣṇā. I take it they consider Drupada's daughter lawfully won. … Draupadī was included within 'everything'; when everything was staked by the eldest of the Pāṇḍavas, how do you consider her not won, when she was won by dharma? She was named aloud, and the Pāṇḍavas consented. On what ground do you hold her to be unwon? … One husband is ordained for a woman by the gods, son of Kuru; but this one is in the power of many, and is therefore determined to be a whore. Bringing her into the hall is nothing remarkable, in my judgment — nor is her being in one garment, nor her being in none."*

> yac caiṣāṃ draviṇaṃ kiṃ cid yā caiṣā ye ca pāṇḍavāḥ
> saubaleneha tat sarvaṃ dharmeṇa vijitaṃ vasu
> duḥśāsana subālo 'yaṃ vikarṇaḥ prājñavādikaḥ
> pāṇḍavānāṃ ca vāsāṃsi draupadyāś cāpy upāhara

*"Whatever property is theirs, and this woman, and the Pāṇḍavas themselves — all of that wealth has been lawfully won here by Saubala. Duḥśāsana: this Vikarṇa is a child talking like a wise man. Take away the garments of the Pāṇḍavas, and Draupadī's too."*

The five brothers strip off their upper garments themselves and sit down in the hall.

> tato duḥśāsano rājan draupadyā vasanaṃ balāt
> sabhāmadhye samākṣipya vyapakraṣṭuṃ pracakrame
> ākṛṣyamāṇe vasane draupadyās tu viśāṃ pate
> tadrūpam aparaṃ vastraṃ prādurāsīd anekaśaḥ
> tato halahalāśabdas tatrāsīd ghoranisvanaḥ
> tad adbhutatamaṃ loke vīkṣya sarvamahīkṣitām

*"Then Duḥśāsana, king, seizing Draupadī's garment by force in the middle of the hall, began to pull it off. And as Draupadī's garment was being pulled, lord of peoples, another garment of the same kind appeared — again and again, many times over. Then there arose a terrible roar of voices there, as all the lords of earth looked upon the greatest wonder in the world."*

The critical edition gives it exactly this much: no invocation, no god named, no explanation. Cloth simply keeps being there. Whether Kṛṣṇa is responsible is a matter the text declines to say; what it says is that at the moment when nobody in the room would act, the world itself refused.

Bhīma swears, in the middle of the kings, his lips shaking with rage, grinding one hand into the other (2.61.44–46):

> idaṃ me vākyam ādaddhvaṃ kṣatriyā lokavāsinaḥ
> noktapūrvaṃ narair anyair na cānyo yad vadiṣyati
> yady etad evam uktvā tu na kuryāṃ pṛthivīśvarāḥ
> pitāmahānāṃ sarveṣāṃ nāhaṃ gatim avāpnuyām
> asya pāpasya durjāter bhāratāpasadasya ca
> na pibeyaṃ balād vakṣo bhittvā ced rudhiraṃ yudhi

*"Take up this word of mine, you warriors who dwell in the world — a thing never spoken before by other men, and which no other will speak after: if, having said this, I do not do it, lords of the earth, may I not attain the destination of my ancestors. If I do not, in battle, break open the chest of this evil man, this low-born one, this outcast of the Bhāratas, and drink his blood by force —"

And when the heap of garments in the middle of the hall was piled high, Duḥśāsana sat down, exhausted and ashamed.

Vidura then raises both arms, stops the assembly, and makes the last formal attempt (2.61.52–57):

> draupadī praśnam uktvaivaṃ roravīti hy anāthavat
> na ca vibrūta taṃ praśnaṃ sabhyā dharmo 'tra pīḍyate
> sabhāṃ prapadyate hy ārtaḥ prajvalann iva havyavāṭ
> taṃ vai satyena dharmeṇa sabhyāḥ praśamayanty uta
> …
> yo hi praśnaṃ na vibrūyād dharmadarśī sabhāṃ gataḥ
> anṛte yā phalāvāptis tasyāḥ so 'rdhaṃ samaśnute
> yaḥ punar vitathaṃ brūyād dharmadarśī sabhāṃ gataḥ
> anṛtasya phalaṃ kṛtsnaṃ saṃprāpnotīti niścayaḥ

*"Draupadī has stated her question and cries out like a woman with no protector — and you do not answer that question. Members of the assembly, dharma is being crushed here. An injured person comes into an assembly like a blazing sacrificial fire, and the members of the assembly quench that fire with truth and with dharma. … A man who sees dharma, who has come into an assembly and does not answer the question, receives half of the fruit that comes of a lie. And a man who sees dharma, who has come into an assembly and answers falsely, obtains the whole fruit of the lie: that is settled."*

He backs it with an old story, Prahlāda's, and Kaśyapa's ruling in it (2.61.67–71):

> jānan na vibruvan praśnaṃ kāmāt krodhāt tathā bhayāt
> sahasraṃ vāruṇān pāśān ātmani pratimuñcati
> tasya saṃvatsare pūrṇe pāśa ekaḥ pramucyate
> …
> viddho dharmo hy adharmeṇa sabhāṃ yatra prapadyate
> na cāsya śalyaṃ kṛntanti viddhās tatra sabhāsadaḥ
> ardhaṃ harati vai śreṣṭhaḥ pādo bhavati kartṛṣu
> pādaś caiva sabhāsatsu ye na nindanti ninditam

*"A man who knows and does not answer the question — from desire, from anger, or from fear — fastens on himself a thousand of Varuṇa's nooses; and one noose is loosed at the end of a full year. … Where dharma comes into an assembly pierced by adharma, and the members of that assembly do not cut the barb out of him, they are themselves pierced. The chief man carries off half the guilt; a quarter falls on the doers; and a quarter on the members of the assembly, who do not condemn what is condemnable."*

The blame is apportioned by fractions, and everyone in the room is holding one.

> vidurasya vacaḥ śrutvā nocuḥ kiṃ cana pārthivāḥ
> karṇo duḥśāsanaṃ tv āha kṛṣṇāṃ dāsīṃ gṛhān naya

*"Having heard Vidura's speech, the kings said nothing whatsoever. But Karṇa said to Duḥśāsana: 'Take Kṛṣṇā, the slave-woman, to the house.'"*

She is dragged again. And she does the one thing left to her, which is to file her grievance in the proper form (2.62.1–2, 8–13):

> purastāt karaṇīyaṃ me na kṛtaṃ kāryam uttaram
> vihvalāsmi kṛtānena karṣatā balinā balāt
> abhivādaṃ karomy eṣāṃ gurūṇāṃ kurusaṃsadi
> na me syād aparādho 'yaṃ yad idaṃ na kṛtaṃ mayā
> …
> kiṃ tv ataḥ kṛpaṇaṃ bhūyo yad ahaṃ strī satī śubhā
> sabhāmadhyaṃ vigāhe 'dya kva nu dharmo mahīkṣitām
> dharmyāḥ striyaḥ sabhāṃ pūrvaṃ na nayantīti naḥ śrutam
> sa naṣṭaḥ kauraveyeṣu pūrvo dharmaḥ sanātanaḥ
> …
> jitāṃ vāpy ajitāṃ vāpi manyadhvaṃ vā yathā nṛpāḥ
> tathā pratyuktam icchāmi tat kariṣyāmi kauravāḥ

*"The duty that I should have performed first, the higher duty, I have not performed; I have been made helpless by this strong man dragging me by force. I make my salutation to these elders in the assembly of the Kurus. Let this not be my fault, that it was not done by me. … What is more wretched than this: that I, a woman, chaste and virtuous, plunge today into the middle of the hall? Where has the dharma of the lords of the earth gone? We have heard that in earlier times righteous men did not bring women into an assembly. That ancient eternal dharma is destroyed among the Kauravas. … Whether you judge me won or not won, kings — I want to be answered on that, and I will do it, Kauravas."*

She has been assaulted in open court, and she is apologizing for having failed to greet her elders first.

Bhīṣma answers a second time and gives up entirely (2.62.14–21):

> uktavān asmi kalyāṇi dharmasya tu parāṃ gatim
> loke na śakyate gantum api viprair mahātmabhiḥ
> balavāṃs tu yathā dharmaṃ loke paśyati pūruṣaḥ
> sa dharmo dharmavelāyāṃ bhavaty abhihitaḥ paraiḥ
> …
> nūnam antaḥ kulasyāsya bhavitā nacirād iva
> tathā hi kuravaḥ sarve lobhamohaparāyaṇāḥ
> …
> ete droṇādayaś caiva vṛddhā dharmavido janāḥ
> śūnyaiḥ śarīrais tiṣṭhanti gatāsava ivānatāḥ
> yudhiṣṭhiras tu praśne 'smin pramāṇam iti me matiḥ

*"I have told you, good woman: the ultimate course of dharma cannot be reached in this world, not even by great brahmins. But whatever a strong man in the world sees as dharma — that, at the moment of dharma's crisis, is what gets called dharma by others. … Surely the end of this family will come, and not long from now, since all the Kurus have gone over to greed and delusion. … These men here, Droṇa and the elders, people who know dharma, sit with empty bodies, bowed like men whose life-breath is gone. But in this question, my judgment is that Yudhiṣṭhira is the authority."*

That middle verse is the darkest sentence in the Sabhā Parva, and it is spoken by the most honorable man in the room: *in a crisis, dharma is whatever the strong man says it is.* He is not endorsing it. He is reporting it. And then he passes the question to a man who has been silent for four chapters.

> tathā tu dṛṣṭvā bahu tat tad evaṃ; rorūyamāṇāṃ kurarīm ivārtām
> nocur vacaḥ sādhv atha vāpy asādhu; mahīkṣito dhārtarāṣṭrasya bhītāḥ

*"Seeing her so, again and again, wailing like an osprey in distress, the lords of earth said not a word, good or bad, in fear of Dhṛtarāṣṭra's son."*

Duryodhana, seeing the silence, smiles and makes an offer (2.62.24–26):

> tiṣṭhatv ayaṃ praśna udārasattve; bhīme 'rjune sahadeve tathaiva
> patyau ca te nakule yājñaseni; vadantv ete vacanaṃ tvatprasūtam
> anīśvaraṃ vibruvantv āryamadhye; yudhiṣṭhiraṃ tava pāñcāli hetoḥ
> kurvantu sarve cānṛtaṃ dharmarājaṃ; pāñcāli tvaṃ mokṣyase dāsabhāvāt

*"Let this question rest on the noble-natured Bhīma, on Arjuna, on Sahadeva, and on your husband Nakula, daughter of Yajñasena; let them speak the word that comes from you. Let them declare in the midst of these nobles, for your sake, Pāñcālī, that Yudhiṣṭhira was not master of you; let them all make the Dharma King a liar, and you will be freed from slavery."*

The trap is exact. Her freedom is available at the price of her husbands publicly calling their eldest brother a liar. Bhīma takes hold of his own arm and answers (2.62.32–37):

> yady eṣa gurur asmākaṃ dharmarājo yudhiṣṭhiraḥ
> na prabhuḥ syāt kulasyāsya na vayaṃ marṣayemahi
> īśo naḥ puṇyatapasāṃ prāṇānām api ceśvaraḥ
> manyate jitam ātmānaṃ yady eṣa vijitā vayam
> na hi mucyeta jīvan me padā bhūmim upaspṛśan
> martyadharmā parāmṛśya pāñcālyā mūrdhajān imān
> …
> dharmapāśasitas tv evaṃ nādhigacchāmi saṃkaṭam
> gauraveṇa niruddhaś ca nigrahād arjunasya ca

*"If this Yudhiṣṭhira, the Dharma King, our elder, were not master of this family, we would not put up with it. He is master of our merits and our austerities, and master even of our lives. If he considers himself won, then we are won. Otherwise no mortal man who had laid hands on these locks of the Pāñcāla woman would get away alive with his foot touching the ground. … But bound like this by the cord of dharma, I find no way through the strait; I am blocked by reverence, and by Arjuna's restraint."*

*Dharmapāśasitaḥ.* Tied by the noose of dharma. It is the same word Vidura used for Varuṇa's nooses and the same word the narrator used for the man bound by the Ordainer. The strongest man alive is described as being roped.

Karṇa makes it worse, being consistent (2.63.1–4):

> trayaḥ kileme adhanā bhavanti; dāsaḥ śiṣyaś cāsvatantrā ca nārī
> dāsasya patnī tvaṃ dhanam asya bhadre; hīneśvarā dāsadhanaṃ ca dāsī
> …
> anyaṃ vṛṇīṣva patim āśu bhāmini; yasmād dāsyaṃ na labhase devanena

*"These three are said to own nothing: a slave, a student, and a woman who is not independent. You are a slave's wife, and his property, good woman; your master is gone, and a slave's property is itself a slave. … Choose another husband quickly, passionate one — one by whom you will not get slavery through dicing."*

Then Duryodhana, laughing, uncovers his left thigh and shows it to her; and Bhīma, his eyes wide and red, swears his second oath in the middle of the kings: *"May Wolf-belly not go to the world of his fathers if I do not split that thigh of yours with my mace in the great battle."*

And then the house answers (2.63.22–26):

> tato rājño dhṛtarāṣṭrasya gehe; gomāyur uccair vyāharad agnihotre
> taṃ rāsabhāḥ pratyabhāṣanta rājan; samantataḥ pakṣiṇaś caiva raudrāḥ

*"Then in the house of king Dhṛtarāṣṭra a jackal howled aloud at the fire-offering; and asses answered it, king, and terrible birds on every side."*

Vidura heard it, and Gāndhārī, and Bhīṣma and Droṇa and Kṛpa cried out "Peace! Peace!" And Gāndhārī and Vidura reported the omen to the king; and the king — who has not spoken since asking *"what is won, what is won"* — finally speaks (2.63.25):

> hato 'si duryodhana mandabuddhe; yas tvaṃ sabhāyāṃ kurupuṃgavānām
> striyaṃ samābhāṣasi durvinīta; viśeṣato draupadīṃ dharmapatnīm

*"You are a dead man, Duryodhana, you dull-witted thing — you who in the assembly of the bulls of the Kurus address a woman so, ill-disciplined creature; and above all Draupadī, a lawful wife."*

It took a jackal. Not his brother, not his teacher, not the woman herself asking four times, not the garments that would not end — an animal noise in the fire-hall.

He turns to Draupadī, conciliating, and offers her a boon. She asks that Yudhiṣṭhira be no slave, so that his son Prativindhya, raised a prince, will not be called a slave's son by other boys. He offers a second. She asks for the four brothers with their chariots and their bows. He offers a third, and she refuses it (2.63.34–36):

> lobho dharmasya nāśāya bhagavan nāham utsahe
> anarhā varam ādātuṃ tṛtīyaṃ rājasattama
> ekam āhur vaiśyavaraṃ dvau tu kṣatrastriyā varau
> trayas tu rājño rājendra brāhmaṇasya śataṃ varāḥ
> pāpīyāṃsa ime bhūtvā saṃtīrṇāḥ patayo mama
> vetsyanti caiva bhadrāṇi rājan puṇyena karmaṇā

*"Greed is the destruction of dharma, lord; I cannot. I do not deserve to take a third boon, best of kings. They say one boon for a vaiśya woman, two for the wife of a warrior, three for a king, and a hundred for a brahmin. My husbands, having become the lowest of men, have been carried across; and they will come to know good things, king, by pure action."*

She counts out the entitlements of her rank and stops at her allotted two. Having just been dragged by the hair through a hall that could not answer her, she declines to take one more than the law allows her.

Karṇa's comment is the last word on it, and — it is characteristic of this epic that this should be so — it is admiring and it is true (2.64.1–3):

> yā naḥ śrutā manuṣyeṣu striyo rūpeṇa saṃmatāḥ
> tāsām etādṛśaṃ karma na kasyāṃ cana śuśrumaḥ
> …
> aplave 'mbhasi magnānām apratiṣṭhe nimajjatām
> pāñcālī pāṇḍuputrāṇāṃ naur eṣā pāragābhavat

*"Of the women famed among men for their beauty, of whom we have heard, we have never heard of such a deed as this from any of them. … For the sons of Pāṇḍu, sunk in water with no boat, going under with no footing anywhere, this Pāñcāla woman became the ship that carried them across."*

Bhīma, who has been silent, says one thing to Arjuna in reply, and it is the sound of a man who has understood exactly what has happened to him: *strī gatiḥ pāṇḍuputrāṇām* — *"A woman is the recourse of the sons of Pāṇḍu."*

Fire with smoke and sparks came out of every opening of his body, out of his ears and the rest, the way flame comes out of the hollows of a burning tree; and his face, in the folds of its frown, was as hard to look at as the face of the Ender when the age has run out. Yudhiṣṭhira held him back with an arm, told him *"not so — sit still"* — and then went and stood before his father, with his palms joined.

*They will be sent home with their kingdom restored. Śakuni will call them back for a second throw before they reach it, and that one will send them to the forest for thirteen years. Everything after — the exile, the failed embassy, Kurukṣetra, the eighteen days, the night killings, the empty field — proceeds from a hall in which several honest men could not answer one question, and from the fact, established in the epic's first pages, that this was what they had all been born for.*

> asyā bhūmer nirasituṃ bhāraṃ bhāgaiḥ pṛthak pṛthak
> asyām eva prasūyadhvaṃ virodhāyeti cābravīt

*"To cast off the burden of this earth, each by his own separate portion — be born upon her, for conflict. So he said."*
